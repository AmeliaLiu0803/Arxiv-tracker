Reliable AI-Assisted Decisions via Selective Auditing
Authors: Isaac Gibbs, Elise L. Han, Ryan J. Tibshirani, Tijana Zrnic
First: 2026-10-22T17:59:22+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-22T17:59:22+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
We consider online monitoring of an AI system that produces a sequence of decisions. At each round, the system outputs a proposed decision together with a confidence score estimating the trustworthiness of the decision. The decision and confidence score need not be produced by the same model: for example, one agent may propose a decision, while a separate verifier agent evaluates it and outputs a correctness or safety score. Based on this information, we must decide whether to accept the AI decision automatically or consult an expert, such as a human auditor, who reviews the proposal and either accepts or corrects it. We formulate this task as an online constrained optimization problem in which the goal is to minimize the frequency of expert auditing while guaranteeing that the fraction of AI errors that go unaudited stays below a user-defined threshold. We derive a family of algorithms with provable error-control guarantees with no assumptions, including a method that is optimally efficient: it asymptotically attains the best achievable auditing rate for both adversarial and i.i.d. data streams. Experiments on screening harmful requests to large language models, qualitative text annotation, and data labeling show that our methods achieve valid error control in practice while substantially reducing the need for human auditing compared to baselines.

We consider online monitoring of an AI system that produces a sequence of decisions.
How Does RL Post-training Induce Skill Composition? A Case Study on Countdown
Authors: Simon Park, Simran Kaur, Sanjeev Arora
First: 2025-12-01T15:17:16+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-22T17:59:20+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
While reinforcement learning (RL) successfully enhances reasoning in large language models, its role in fostering compositional generalization (the ability to synthesize novel skills from known components) is often conflated with mere length generalization. To this end, we study what RL post-training teaches about skill composition and how the composition structure affects the skill transfer. We focus on the Countdown task (given n numbers and a target, form an expression that evaluates to the target) and analyze model solutions as expression trees, where each subtree corresponds to a reusable subtask and thus can be viewed as a "skill." Tracking tree shapes and their success rates over training, we find: (i) out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization to larger n and to unseen tree shapes, indicating compositional reuse of subtasks; (ii) the order of skill acquisition depends upon the composition structure---models master shallow balanced trees (workload is balanced between subtasks) before deep unbalanced ones, with persistent fragility on right-heavy structures for a fixed composition depth. Our diagnostic reveals what is learned, in what order, and where generalization fails, clarifying how RL-only post-training induces OOD generalization beyond what standard metrics such as pass@k reveal. To study the implications of our findings for real-world tasks, we present a preliminary study on formal theorem proving.

Cost-Aware Abstention for LLM-Based IT Ticket Classification
Authors: Sachin Gupta
First: 2026-10-22T17:58:52+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-22T17:58:52+00:00
Comments: Accepted at the 38th IEEE International Conference on Tools with Artificial Intelligence (ICTAI 2026). 9 pages, 5 figures, 9 tables
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Wrong automatic routing of an IT support ticket can cost more than human triage. We evaluate cost-aware abstention as an offline, cost-conditional readiness assessment: select a routing policy on calibration data under explicit costs, then evaluate that frozen policy once on held-out test data. On a public 47,837-ticket, eight-class corpus, we compare four trained non-LLM classifiers with four large language models under zero- and few-shot prompting. Under the illustrative cost model (c_ok, c_triage, c_wrong) = (0, 1, 5), TF-IDF with logistic regression and a threshold of 0.60 selected on calibration reduces test cost from 0.763 to 0.472, a 38.1% relative reduction (paired ratio-bootstrap 95% CI [35.7%, 40.4%]), while lowering wrong routes from 15.3% to 3.5%. Frozen calibration-selected policies also beat both always-route and always-defer on held-out test data in 14 of 16 settings in a post-hoc cost-parameter analysis. The strongest prompted configuration reaches 0.477 accuracy on a stratified 1,000-ticket test subset, versus 0.879 for fine-tuned DistilBERT on the same tickets. Isotonic lowers expected calibration error without reliably improving discrimination: GPT-4o-mini few-shot has one confidence value before and after recalibration and correctness AUROC 0.500. We therefore report correctness AUROC and tie-aware risk-coverage area alongside ECE. At the analytically derived break-even probability 0.80, seven prompted configurations defer all tickets; Haiku few-shot routes only 19/1,000. An LLM-only agreement control and margin/entropy rejection baselines further separate calibration from discrimination. The findings are scoped to this dataset, prompt design, model endpoints, and illustrative costs; the work is an evaluation framework, not a new abstention algorithm or universal deployment certification.

Wrong automatic routing of an IT support ticket can cost more than human triage.
Beyond Naturalness: Probing Automated Text-To-Speech Evaluators on Linguistically Grounded Dimensions

Venue: EMNLP 2026

Abstract
Automated Text-to-Speech (TTS) evaluation methods (Mean Opinion Score (MOS) predictors and Audio Large Language Model (Audio-LLM) judges) are expected to reflect human perception, yet it is unclear how well they capture the distinct aspects of speech that listeners actually perceive. We deconstruct "naturalness" into a linguistically grounded annotation schema spanning 10 distinct perceptual dimensions, and use it to construct the first dimension-level meta-evaluation benchmark for TTS, comprising 860 utterances annotated by trained linguist raters. Results from benchmarking four MOS predictors and four Audio-LLM judges reveal that MOS predictors are more sensitive to acoustic signal-level properties at the prosodic and paralinguistic levels, while Audio-LLM judges show selective, prompt-dependent detection that favors word-level errors and does not generalize across all dimensions. Neither class reliably captures a breadth of linguistically structured, human-perceptible speech errors. Our dataset, annotation schema, and evaluation code are publicly released to support more targeted and interpretable TTS evaluation.

Abstract
Institutions that deploy large language models in regulated settings often need technical evidence of responsible AI practices before approving a deployment. COMPL-AI provides a technical interpretation of the EU AI Act that organises this evidence under six principle groups and eighteen technical requirements, twelve of which current benchmarks can measure. The benchmark suites that produce this evidence are typically operated from a command line, while the staff who approve deployments are non-engineers. We present VERA, an open-source self-hosted dashboard deployed to facilitate responsible AI processes within a large European bank. A reviewer selects a model and runs public benchmarks against it. VERA scores the twelve requirements that current benchmarks can measure and instruments the energy consumed by each evaluation, adding run-level evidence for environmental impact. We run four served open-weight models through the requirement pipeline, and the dashboard displays a score for each measurable requirement. A within-subject study then compares how participants read these benchmark results: from the raw output files in one condition, and through the VERA dashboard in the other. The dashboard reduced the time needed to perform the responsible AI assessment, reducing the time required from 56 seconds using raw files to 30 seconds using our dashboard, on average. Our dashboard scored highly (4.36 out of 5) on usefulness in the survey conducted within the organization. The dashboard and the study material are released publicly to promote replicability and reproducibility in different organisational contexts.

Who Is Sycophantic to Whom? Confident LLMs, Human Deference, and the Collapse of Trust Calibration
Authors: Deepthika S, Pawni Yadav, B. Sankar
First: 2026-10-22T17:15:59+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-22T17:15:59+00:00
Comments: 49 pages; 32 figures; 7 tables
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Research on sycophancy asks whether large language models (LLMs) bend towards what their users want to hear. We ask the reverse question: when an LLM is confident, consistent, and wrong, do users bend towards it? In a within-subjects experiment, 32 participants learned up to five science topics by chatting with five assistants built on the same model (GPT-5-mini). Unknown to participants, each assistant's briefing had 0, 25, 50, 75, or 100% of its facts replaced by plausible falsehoods, and every assistant spoke with full confidence. A surprise quiz after each topic offered the correct fact, the planted falsehood, and two distractors, with a confidence rating for every answer. Across 104 quizzes and 1,040 answers, scores fell from 7.4 to 2.9 out of 10, and the planted option was chosen on 60% of falsified items against 11% of correctly taught ones (odds ratio 12.5). Transcript coding showed that whenever the assistant stated a falsehood, it was adopted about 65% of the time at every falsification level. Absorbed falsehoods were held with high confidence (8.4/10), the modeled confidence advantage of right over wrong answers vanished, and the discrimination of confidence for correctness fell from an AUROC of 0.70 to chance, while post-task trust ratings barely moved. Participants tended to return to the chat more when the assistant was worse, but returning went with more absorption, not less, and 30% of all turns pasted quiz questions back to the source being checked. The assistant itself was almost never sycophantic and never conceded under pushback. We introduce a baseline-corrected Human Deference Index to quantify this human-side sycophancy and argue that trustworthiness is a property of the human-AI pair, that sycophancy evaluations should measure both directions, and that learning interfaces must make reliability legible claim by claim.

Research on sycophancy asks whether large language models (LLMs) bend towards what their users want to hear.
Rethinking Sequence Compression with Test-time Regression
Authors: Sihan Yang, Jie Huang, Hanxuan Li, Yifan Guo, Weigao Sun, Yiran Zhong, Weiyang Liu
First: 2026-10-22T17:14:38+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-22T17:14:38+00:00
Comments: Technical report (19 pages, 4 figures, project page: https://spherelab.ai/ttr-compressor)
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Sequence compression is a fundamental operation and a critical information bottleneck in efficient language and multimodal models, and yet prevailing approaches based on pooling or learned projections remain underperforming. We revisit sequence compression through the lens of test-time regression: input tokens define a regression problem whose fitted coefficients form the compressed representation. Importantly, the regression problem is learned end-to-end via backpropagation, while its coefficients are computed efficiently in closed form during the forward pass. This leads to TTR-Compressor, a general alternative to conventional pooling and projection-based compression. We evaluate TTR-Compressor as the compressor for block-wise sparse attention and the visual token merger for multimodal large language models. On block-wise sparse attention, TTR-Compressor improves RULER performance over attention-based compressors by a large margin. For visual token merging, it consistently outperforms the standard MLP merger and matches its performance with 55.6% fewer visual tokens. These results establish test-time regression as an effective primitive for sequence compression in efficient language and multimodal models.

Abstract
In the post-API landscape, social-media profiling has become inexpensive and automated: Large Language Models (LLMs) can extract structured information from unstructured content and deduce attributes users never disclosed. A person's true exposure is not what they post but what can be inferred from it. As these practices become routine, exposure to social engineering attacks becomes an operational certainty, demanding a shared framework for assessment and mitigation. We make this exposure measurable by quantifying how accurately a target can be profiled, and how far that profile enables social engineering attacks. Because no existing model fully spans this continuum, we unify Social Media Intelligence (SOCMINT) and Cyber Human Intelligence (Cyber-HUMINT), two individual-focused disciplines that evolved separately, into a single doctrine-grounded taxonomy of 38 capabilities across the Cyber Threat Intelligence lifecycle, where user profiling bridges passive collection and active engagement. Domain experts validated the taxonomy (Fleiss' $κ$ = 0.92). We operationalize its core path (extraction, inference, and susceptibility assessment) as an LLM-augmented multi-agent pipeline, evaluated on real participants' public data. Susceptibility is assessed indirectly: the pipeline predicts how each target would rank personalized engagement options, matching their actual choices significantly above chance. This ranking capacity captures the personalization on which tailored attacks depend. The underlying profiles are accurate: extraction reaches F1 = 97.3%, and inferred attributes score 4.73/5, both far above rule-based and NER-based baselines at negligible cost. The framework augments security analysts' tradecraft rather than replacing it and enables defenders to gauge their own exposure before an adversary does.

Projecting Latent RL Actions: Towards Generalizable and Scalable Graph Combinatorial Optimization
Authors: Franco Terranova, Guillermo Bernardez, Albert Cabellos-Aparicio, Nina Miolane, Abdelkader Lahmadi
First: 2026-05-19T11:55:44+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-22T17:10:07+00:00
Comments: Preprint
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Graph combinatorial optimization (GCO) has attracted growing interest, as many NP-hard problems naturally admit graph formulations, yet their combinatorial explosion renders exact methods computationally intractable. Recent advances in Reinforcement Learning (RL) combined with Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have significantly improved learning-based GCO solvers. However, existing approaches face limitations in both generalization across diverse graph instances and computational scalability as action spaces grow. To address both challenges, we introduce \textit{projection agents}, a GCO methodology built on the Wolpertinger framework and adapted to a shared GNN-based latent space for observations and actions. These agents are trained to solve GCO problems by predicting a desired latent action in a single forward pass, which is subsequently decoded into a valid discrete action in the graph using the latent space. Across diverse benchmarks, our approach achieves up to 6.41$\times$ faster inference and up to 75\% better generalization than existing solutions using a simple nearest-neighbor decoding, while opening the door to strong RL performance in super-linear decision spaces with multiple interdependent variables. Finally, we release \texttt{LaGCO-RL}, a Python library that automates latent action-space construction and supports existing RL-GCO solutions, promoting reproducibility and adaptation to new GCO benchmarks.

Abstract
Industrial organizations hold operational knowledge in structured records, such as telemetry, point lists, and transactions, and in unstructured sources, such as manuals, service notes, communications, and inspection reports. Governance programs often manage these sources separately. Classifying, linking, and documenting them depends on a small group of employees with deep domain knowledge. Agents built on large language models can propose metadata, cite documents, and flag quality issues, but unreviewed proposals can spread errors into downstream systems. A perfunctory approval leaves no record of what a reviewer checked. We present contestable governance: specialist agents produce scored, cited arguments for disputed metadata decisions; a steward can accept, reject, modify, or add arguments before commit; and a governance record retains the evidence, interventions, and outcome. A versioned domain glossary supplies model-specific terms and acronym definitions to the agents. We describe the workflow, a triage path that reserves full argumentation for uncertain proposals while preserving review before commit, the scoring and record models, and a gate that controls which verified decisions become precedent. Two HVAC use cases illustrate the pattern: point classification using Brick Schema class labels, supported by an open reference implementation, and an illustrative warranty failure-mode decision linking claim fields and telemetry to service notes and revisioned bulletins.

Abstract
Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) stands as a fundamental challenge in robotics, essential for enabling autonomous agents to execute complex, long-horizon tasks in diverse environments. While Large Language Models (LLMs) introduce a new paradigm for TAMP by leveraging extensive common sense, their inherent reasoning limitations hinder direct application, and fine-tuning strategies to enhance these capabilities are currently bottlenecked by the scarcity of high-quality datasets. Semantics Delivery Network: Rethinking Web Retrieval Infrastructure for LLM Agents

First: 2026-09-18T18:49:38+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-22T16:21:58+00:00
Comments: Accepted and to appear at the 25th ACM Workshop on Hot Topics in Networks (HotNets 2026). 13 pages, 3 figures
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) increasingly ground their answers in proprietary data and live web content through both single-shot retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and multi-turn agentic search. Yet the web path they use was built for human readers: search services return URLs and snippets ranked by generic relevance, and content delivery networks (CDNs) cache URL-addressed objects without knowing which passage an agent needs. LLMs instead consume short, semantically coherent passages - hereafter, chunks - selected for downstream utility rather than similarity alone, retrieve statefully across reasoning turns, and, as uncoordinated streams, repeatedly fetch, clean, chunk, and embed the same content. We argue that semantic chunk retrieval should become a first-class network-delivery abstraction, and propose the Semantics Delivery Network (SemDN): an origin-authorized, hierarchical substrate that indexes, searches, and caches web content at chunk granularity, serving clients and origins alike. SemDN is more than finer-grained caching: it must estimate missing or stale evidence without an explicit miss signal, let clients choose retrieval representations that origins need not see, and share state among clients with the same representation. Preliminary results show that page-first retrieval processes two to three orders of magnitude more content than agents consume, that agentic queries exhibit task-local chunk reuse invisible to URL caches, and that chunk delivery improves answer quality per context token. We organize an open research agenda around shareable retrieval state, hierarchical caching and refresh under coverage risk, and deployability, including incentives, privacy, and verifiability. Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for molecular tasks, but it remains unclear which molecular representation to use. We present a systematic benchmark, MolRepBench, to evaluate LLM molecular competence across nine representations and eight chemical tasks. We benchmark 16 LLMs across five model families, including reasoning and non-reasoning variants, chemistry-specialized LLMs, and closed frontier models. Performance is strongly representation-dependent and no single representation wins across tasks, though CML is the best, followed by MolJSON, InChI and canonical SMILES. Explicit structured text representations (CML and MolJSON) dominate structural tasks; IUPAC dominates semantic tasks, winning molecule retrieval for all 16 LLMs; and SMILES variants are rarely optimal despite their prevalence in pretraining. Using LLM-as-a- judge, we find that IUPAC produces the highest fraction of correct molecule generations. A mechanistic study via tokenization audits, linear probes, and attention shows that representations are encoded differently inside the model; for example, structured representations require more attention across the molecular span. Our results argue against representation-invariant evaluation and motivate task-aware representation routing for LLM-based chemistry.

Abstract
Recent advances in Reinforcement Post-Training (RPT) have substantially improved the mathematical reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs), but it remains unclear how well such gains transfer across languages. In this work, we study cross-lingual mathematical reasoning transfer in English-centric LLMs under controlled multilingual evaluation. We first evaluate a range of English-centric reasoning models on multilingual mathematical reasoning benchmarks and find that transfer is highly variable across initial model choice, target language, and training paradigm. Finally, we show that the largest improvement comes from moving beyond English-only supervision: adding a single parallel language yields a substantial gain, while further gains from additional languages are smaller but consistent. Overall, our results highlight the need to evaluate multilingual mathematical reasoning directly, using both absolute performance and relative transfer-efficiency metrics.

Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) differ in capabilities and costs, motivating routing methods that adaptively choose a model for each query. These methods rely on performance prediction: given a query, estimate whether a model will answer it correctly. Most routing methods train separate predictors of candidate models' performance to guide model selection. Our work shows that performance prediction only beats a simple baseline when the candidate model is suboptimal on the task or the performance predictor adds significant capacity relative to the candidate model. Specifically, we take a model's self-confidence, the probability it assigns to its chosen answer, as a baseline and study how much a performance predictor can improve on it. First, we show that any performance predictor that outperforms a model's self-confidence can be used to improve the model's loss: the predictor's advantage transfers exactly in log loss through recalibration. Second, accounting for the predictor's additional parameters, we bound its advantage by the model's suboptimality plus the gain from those parameters. Third, we extend our theoretical results to two-model cascades, which route queries to a more capable model when the first model is predicted to fail. Experiments with 16 language models on four benchmarks show that predictors gain a larger advantage over self-confidence on more suboptimal models. This advantage shrinks as models improve through finetuning. Cascading exhibits the same two patterns: predictor-based routing offers larger gains over confidence-based routing for more suboptimal models, and these gains diminish after finetuning.

Abstract

Abstract

Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly use persistent memory to personalize responses, and personal attributes in this memory pose a privacy risk if exposed. We ask how consulting memory during dialogue changes what users go on to say, and thus what the memory comes to reveal. In multi-session simulations, a simulated user with 24 personal attributes talks with a support agent that does or does not consult memory, and in both settings attributes are inferred from the full accumulated record. Across four support models, memory raises response quality but also empirical leakage, the share of attribute values correctly inferred from the record, by a margin that widens over sessions.
