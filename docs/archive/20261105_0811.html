Adversarial Speculative Reasoning Improves Long Horizon Agents
Authors: Hongyin Luo, Wei Fang, Lijie Yang, Owen Stepan, Daniel Suit, Jeff Krapf, Dana Wensberg, Jack O'Brien
First: 2026-11-03T18:45:23+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T18:45:23+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Speculative decoding has been treated purely as a systems optimization that speeds up large language model decoding without changing the model behavior. Under this view, the growing effort to train better draft models has been focused solely on acceptance rate. We argue that this view does not hold true for long-horizon agent reasoning. Measuring frontier open-weight models on complex agentic tasks with different speculative decoding approaches, we found that numerical noise grows within verification windows, and the draft acceptance results of the thinking process can change the output distribution of the model after 30 steps, thus noticeably influencing the reasoning accuracy of long-horizon agents in random directions. In this work, we improve the reasoning capability of target models by controlling such noise. While we cannot change the overall token distribution verified by the target model, we can encode critical reasoning with highest precision by training the draft model to be rejected. We identify these points in agent traces and train the draft model to favor adversarial preference. At inference the trained draft and the target part ways precisely at these decision points, verification restarts, so that critical thinking tokens are sampled from the target model and encoded under its least noisy conditions. This approach makes critical reasoning better indexed and more durable over a long horizon. On complex agentic coding benchmarks, our approach raises end-to-end success rate with no change to the target model or its output distribution. Our results recast the speculative decoding mechanism from a pure accelerator into an active allocator for context precision that improves the accuracy of long-horizon agents by training 0.06% to 0.28% of hosted parameters.

Abstract
The Large Language Model from Power Law Decoder Representations (PLDR-LLM) and its attention, Power Law Graph Attention (PLGA), replace the fixed bilinear form of scaled dot-product attention (SDPA) with a learned, input-generated bilinear operator $G_{\mathrm{LM}}$, built from a positive tensor $A_{\mathrm{LM}}$ by elementwise power laws. Unconditionally: PLGA contains SDPA exactly at $G_{\mathrm{LM}}=I$; $A_{\mathrm{LM}}$ and $A_{P}$ are strictly entrywise positive, with Perron-Frobenius structure on $A_{\mathrm{LM}}$; the DAG regularizer has the NOTEARS walk-counting form and positivity obstructs exact acyclicity; and, under nonresonance (satisfied by standard rotary frequencies), a commutant criterion identifies which operators preserve relative-position dependence. Knowingly Wrong: Training LLMs Against Their Own Beliefs can Induce Misalignment
Authors: Ariana M. Villegas-Suarez, Guido Freire, Sohan Venkatesh, Bartosz Cywiński
First: 2026-11-03T18:02:21+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T18:02:21+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Fine-tuning a large language model (LLM) on harmful data in a narrow domain, such as bad medical advice, can make it broadly misaligned. How realistic this risk is remains unclear, since models are very rarely fine-tuned on explicitly harmful data in practice. In this work, we show that fine-tuning on data the model believes to be false but that has no harmful content, can also induce broad misalignment. Across mathematics, trivia, cooking and sports, the more strongly the model disbelieves the answers it is trained on, the more misaligned it becomes. In mathematics, misaligned answers rise from 4% to over 60% as the training answers become more wrong, with little loss of general capability. After fine-tuning, the model states the wrong answers but its multiple-choice probabilities still prefer the true ones, which suggests that misalignment comes from this internal inconsistency. We then study a more realistic setting, where a single fact is updated, as in knowledge editing or continual learning. The same relationship holds and the more the model still believes the true fact after training on the false one, the more misaligned it becomes. When we frame the same false fact as part of a coherent story, the model comes to believe it more deeply and becomes less misaligned than when we instruct it to assert the false fact. Content filters and persona vectors cannot tell these two framings apart, even though they lead to different levels of misalignment. These results suggest a risk for model updates, where training a model that does not adopt the new fact as true can induce misalignment even when the data looks harmless.

The Geometry of Ignorance: Detecting Suppressed Knowledge in LLMs through Activation Fragmentation
Authors: Adar Peleg, Dvir Alsheich, Tomer Ashuach, Avi Mendelson
Venue: EMNLP
First: 2026-11-03T17:55:57+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T17:55:57+00:00
Comments: Presented at EMNLP Findings 2026
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Evaluating whether a large language model (LLM) genuinely knows a concept remains a fundamental problem, since behavioral metrics can be fooled by suppression: a model that refuses to answer may still harbor recoverable knowledge. The framework measures the geometric coherence of \textit{anon-vectors}, the differences in hidden states between a concept-specific query and its anonymized paraphrase, and shows that genuine knowledge forms a tight, low-entropy cluster while ignorance forces geometric fragmentation. Across ten instruction-tuned LLMs from three model families, all fragmentation metrics reliably separate known from unknown concepts, and post-unlearning, fragmentation geometry correlates with adversarial recovery across four attack types and five unlearning algorithms, though the direction of that relationship is shown to depend on attack methodology. Furthermore, a layer-resolved analysis reveals that different adversarial mechanisms exploit this geometry at varying depths, with high attack-specific correlations that are invisible to behavioral evaluation. Together, these results constitute a single-phase, attack-free proxy for unlearning robustness that captures aspects of parametric knowledge inaccessible to output-level metrics. A reference implementation is available at https://github.com/AdarPeleg/geometry-of-ignorance

vLLM Hook v1: A Plug-in for Programming Model Internals on vLLM
Authors: Ching-Yun Ko, Yunhua Fang, Pin-Yu Chen
First: 2026-02-02T16:34:13+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T17:53:11+00:00

Abstract
Modern artificial intelligence models are deployed on inference engines to optimize runtime efficiency and resource allocation, particularly for transformer-based large language models. The vLLM project is a major open-source library to support model serving and inference. However, the current implementation of vLLM limits programmability of the internal states of deployed models. This prevents the use of popular test-time model alignment and enhancement methods. For example, it prevents the detection of adversarial prompts based on attention patterns or the adjustment of model responses based on activation steering. To bridge this critical gap, we present vLLM Hook, an opensource plug-in to enable the programming of internal states for vLLM models. Based on a configuration file specifying which internal states to capture, vLLM Hook provides seamless integration to vLLM and supports two essential features: passive programming and active programming. For passive programming, vLLM Hook probes the selected internal states for subsequent analysis, while keeping the model generation intact. For active programming, vLLM Hook enables efficient intervention of model generation by altering the selected internal states. In version 1 (named vLLM Hook MIA), the plugin runs on vLLM's V2 model runner and preserves the engine's CUDA graphs, so that capturing and steering internal states incurs only a small decoding overhead. It supports both offline use and serving through vllm serve. In addition to presenting the core functions of vLLM Hook, we demonstrate use cases including prompt injection detection, attention-based re-ranking for retrieval-augmented generation, activation steering, hidden-state probing, hallucination detection, schema linking for text-to-SQL, and tool-call risk assessment. Finally, we welcome the community's contribution to improve vLLM Hook via https://github.com/IBM/vLLM-Hook.

JHiMA-WebRL: A Joint Hierarchical Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning Framework for Long-Horizon Web Tasks
Authors: Shanka Subhra Mondal, Jiayun Wang
First: 2026-11-03T17:49:14+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T17:49:14+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Multimodal web agents have shown promising capabilities for interacting with websites through graphical user interfaces (GUIs), yet realistic web tasks often require long-horizon, multi-step planning that remains challenging for monolithic policies. Such agents must jointly infer high-level plans, navigate through intermediate states, track progress over extended interaction histories, and execute visually grounded actions. Although prior work has explored modular multi-agent systems with a separate planner, the planner is typically not updated jointly with the actor. We investigate whether explicitly separating and jointly training planning and execution provides a stronger inductive bias for long-horizon web navigation. We introduce JHiMA-WebRL, a joint hierarchical multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for web agents that couples a task decomposer with an actor. To train the decomposer, we use a strong teacher model to construct structured subtask supervision from multimodal GUI trajectories, and use these annotations to fine-tune both the decomposer and the subtask-conditioned actor. We then extend Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to jointly optimize the decomposer and actor in the interactive WebArena environment through hierarchical rollout grouping and role-specific advantages. On WebArena-Lite-v2, JHiMA-WebRL-14B, consisting of a 7B actor and a 7B decomposer, achieves an average success rate of 37.2%, compared with 24% for a ScaleCUA-32B agent that we fine-tune with RL on the same training tasks and evaluate under the same protocol. Ablations show that jointly updating the decomposer during RL outperforms updating the actor alone. These results suggest that jointly learned role factorization with smaller models can be a competitive alternative to scaling monolithic agents for long-horizon web tasks.

Zero Shot Coordination for Sparse Reward Tasks with Diverse Reward Shapings
Authors: Keenan Powell, Peihong Yu, Pratap Tokekar
First: 2026-04-28T00:09:28+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T16:58:18+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Many Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) agents fail to adapt properly to cooperating with agents trained with the same objectives but different seeds, algorithms, or other training differences. This is the problem of Zero-Shot Coordination (ZSC), which focuses on training agents to cooperate well with unknown agents. ZSC has been studied for a variety of tabular cases and simple games such as Hanabi, achieving excellent results. However, existing solutions to ZSC only consider identical rewards for your trained agents and all future partners. This is not realistic for the trained agents, as they do not consider the problem of cooperating with agents that have identical sparse objectives but shape the rewards for those objectives in different manner. To address this issue, we show how to train an ensemble of methods using randomized reward shapings chosen using 4 selection algorithms. Experiments done on the Overcooked environment demonstrate consistent improvements of 62.2%-119.2% in sparse reward over baseline ZSC algorithms when playing with agents that have identical sparse rewards but different reward shapings.

Abstract
The generative artificial intelligence (AI) ecosystem is undergoing rapid transformations that threaten its sustainability. As models transition from research prototypes to high-traffic products, the energetic burden has shifted from one-time training to recurring, unbounded inference. This is exacerbated by reasoning models that inflate compute costs by orders of magnitude per query. The prevailing pursuit of artificial general intelligence through scaling of monolithic models is colliding with hard physical constraints: grid failures, water consumption, and diminishing returns on data scaling. This trajectory yields models with impressive factual recall but struggles in domains requiring in-depth reasoning, possibly due to insufficient abstractions in training data. Current large language models (LLMs) exhibit genuine reasoning depth only in domains like mathematics and coding, where rigorous, pre-existing abstractions provide structural grounding. In other fields, the current approach fails to generalize well. We propose an alternative trajectory based on domain-specific superintelligence (DSS). We argue for first constructing explicit symbolic abstractions (knowledge graphs, ontologies, and formal logic) to underpin synthetic curricula enabling small language models to master domain-specific reasoning without the model collapse problem typical of LLM-based synthetic data methods. Rather than a single generalist giant model, we envision "societies of DSS models": dynamic ecosystems where orchestration agents route tasks to distinct DSS back-ends. This paradigm shift decouples capability from size, enabling intelligence to migrate from energy-intensive data centers to secure, on-device experts. By aligning algorithmic progress with physical constraints, DSS societies move generative AI from an environmental liability to a sustainable force for economic empowerment.

CONFIRM: Governing Scientific Reporting for Trustworthy Agentic Neuroimaging
Authors: Yuxiang Wei, Lijing Zhu, Qizhen Lan, Yanteng Zhang, Vince D. Calhoun
First: 2026-11-01T02:37:56+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-03T16:50:14+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents can autonomously run neuroimaging analyses from research questions to reported findings. Existing agentic systems use tools to check that code runs and reported values match its output, but rely on LLM judgment to decide which results warrant scientific reporting. We show that LLM judges endorse site-confounded findings even when the confound is presented as evidence. We present \textbf{CONFIRM}, a framework that separates running an analysis from deciding which findings to report. CONFIRM encodes each hypothesis as a machine-readable, pre-specified analysis plan that fixes the claim and its pass criteria before computing results. It then filters results through five deterministic gates (search history and multiplicity, confounds, power, analytic stability, and replication) under three configurable reporting standards, from least to most demanding: \textit{Screen}, \textit{Replicate}, and \textit{Full}. Failed claims receive diagnoses that guide follow-up analyses. To evaluate reporting decisions at the claim level, we introduce \textbf{NeuroClaimBench}, comprising 489 executable neuroimaging claims with frozen data, including literature-supported findings and 150 controls designed to violate individual methodological requirements. On NeuroClaimBench, CONFIRM falsely supports at most 3\% of controls, compared with 23--38\% for the baselines, while recovering up to 61\% of literature-supported claims. When auditing an existing agent, CONFIRM reduces false support on controls from 23\% to 0\% while retaining the agent's literature-supported claims. Its diagnostic feedback also produces more supported revisions than Self-Refine with fewer LLM calls. Together, CONFIRM and NeuroClaimBench show that basing reporting decisions on pre-specified analysis plans and deterministic gates is essential for trustworthy autonomous scientific discovery.

Abstract
OperAItor, an open-source framework for developing and evaluating large language model agents for supervisory fault management in chemical processes. The framework is demonstrated on a dynamic reactor-distillation process representing isopropanol dehydrogenation, with physically based thermodynamics and modified reaction kinetics to permit liquid-phase operation. The process is represented by a 113-variable differential-algebraic model and operates at a nominal feed rate of 100kmol/h, producing approximately 4t/h of acetone. The simulated plant includes 8 PI control loops, 22 process alarms, 5 duty-standby pump trains, and 11 independent safety instrumented functions. The agent architecture integrates multivariate statistical process monitoring, fault diagnosis, knowledge-graph retrieval, action planning, and supervisory action generation. Five action-validation configurations are investigated, comprising operation without validation, an LLM-based judge, digital-twin simulation, and deterministic admissibility checks with and without consequence projection. The framework is evaluated using four LLMs across 20 scenarios involving single and multiple simultaneous faults. Without agent intervention, 18 scenarios result in plant stops, corresponding to an economic loss of $399,000 relative to fault-free operation under the assumed benchmark economics. For GPT-5 nano, Qwen3 8B, and Mistral 7B, validated agent configurations reduce the number of stops to 5-8 and recover $265,000 of the loss. The study provides a reproducible benchmark for comparing agent architectures, language models, and action-validation methods for supervisory fault management in chemical processes.

Abstract

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as autonomous agents that negotiate, coordinate, and make decisions on behalf of human users. Understanding how they cooperate is therefore important for AI governance and machine behaviour research. We examine two factors: payoff magnitude and interaction language. Classical game theory predicts that multiplying all payoffs by a positive constant should not change behaviour because the underlying strategic incentives remain unchanged. We test this prediction using five widely adopted commercial LLMs playing an iterated prisoner's dilemma across ten payoff scales and five languages (English, French, Vietnamese, Chinese and Arabic). Contrary to theoretical expectations, changing only the numerical scale of payoffs substantially alters cooperation. Models respond differently, often in opposite directions, meaning that averages across models can conceal important behavioural heterogeneity. Sensitivity to payoff scale also depends strongly on prompt language. Moreover, payoff scale modifies the effects of persona instructions: at sub-unit stakes, where no payoff shown exceeds 1, all five models cooperate more under a selfish than a cooperative persona, whereas Claude and Gemini reverse this pattern at larger scales. These findings show that cooperation measures depend critically on payoff scale and language and should not be treated as fixed model traits.

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being used to turn citizens' opinions into collective proposals. These proposals can make assignments: who should act, what must be done, how much one should spend and by when. However, if the assignments change when the same opinions are processed again, publishing a single proposal can hide alternatives that participants never get to see. We examined this issue in four LLM mediators (three open-weight and one commercial) using opinions from DeliberationBank and Polis. For each task, a mediator drafted a proposal from thirty opinions five times, varying either the random seed or the order of the opinions. In 17% of five-run sets from the open-weight mediators and 20% from the commercial one, some runs contained assignments while others did not. Reordering the opinions changed the assignments almost as often as replacing them with opinions from different citizens. Substantial variation remained under four safeguards: temperature zero, a commercial mediator, draft selection, and draft merging. The mediator's objective also shaped representation: prompts seeking agreement covered minority positions much less often than prompts asking to describe differences. A single proposal can therefore obscure choices in both the assignments it proposes and the views it excludes. We propose audits that make alternative assignments visible and disclose the objective behind each proposal.

Abstract
Speech large language models retain audio history to support multi-turn conversations. Yet remembering an event does not ensure that its information is used only when relevant. We investigate why irrelevant historical audio changes answers even when the queried event remains represented. Controlled experiments on MOSS-Audio and MiMo-Audio show that historical values direct errors toward specific competing answers, beyond a generic effect of context length. Causal interventions distinguish two paths. In open-ended question answering, most history-value errors disappear when subsequent computation cannot directly access the irrelevant source. Reliable conversational memory therefore requires not only retention and retrieval, but also control over how retained information enters answer computation.

Abstract

Abstract
Pervasive Capability Degradation (PCD) refers to the phenomenon in which supervised fine-tuning (SFT), while adapting a large language model (LLM) to a target task, often erodes the general capabilities acquired during pre-training.
