CPU-ARC: Agentic Reasoning Caching for on-CPU Reasoning Reuse
Authors: Peter Belcak, Greg Heinrich, Saurav Muralidharan, Shizhe Diao, Pavlo Molchanov
First: 2026-11-06T18:58:50+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-06T18:58:50+00:00
Comments: Tech report. 28 pages, 9 figures, 11 tables
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language models reinvent the wheel all the time. They conflate task-solving with the execution of the solutions, which forces them to solve every problem from scratch. We show that once a task has been reasoned through, the application of its solution to a new problem instance can be done very efficiently and with little involvement from language models. We introduce agentic reasoning caching, a method for constructing robust CPU-executable agentic reasoning caches (ARCs) from LLM reasoning traces. ARCs show on-par performance with frontier LLMs across a range of difficult mathematical and scientific tasks, are fully explainable, continually trainable, robust to input perturbations, and 16-291x cheaper to invoke than the source model.

Grasp-TAGS: Task-Aware Grasp Selection via Critic Distillation for Prehensile Loco-Manipulation
Authors: Xun Tu, Minghao Zou, Karthik Desingh
First: 2026-11-06T18:44:33+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-06T18:44:33+00:00
Comments: 10 pages, 6 figures
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Loco-manipulation, which requires mobile manipulators to coordinate locomotion and manipulation over long horizons, is a key capability for large-object rearrangement in applications such as service robotics and warehouse automation. Despite recent progress, reliably executing these tasks remains challenging because performance depends on complex robot-object and object-environment interaction dynamics. Existing approaches address this challenge either through model-based methods, which often require accurate dynamics models, or through learning-based methods, which reduce this requirement but typically assume a fixed or predefined initial grasp. However, different geometrically stable grasps can lead to substantially different downstream manipulation performance. Therefore, we propose Grasp-TAGS, a task-aware grasp selection framework for goal-conditioned large-object rearrangement. We train a unified PPO-based reinforcement learning policy across multiple initial grasp configurations and distill the learned critic into a lightweight grasp utility network that ranks candidate grasps using only the candidate grasp configuration and goal information. At inference time, we select the grasp with the highest predicted downstream utility for the downstream policy to complete the task, before even making a contact. Experiments demonstrate that Grasp-TAGS improves rearrangement success over both fixed single-grasp policies and random grasp selection, achieving an average success rate of 89.84% compared with 79.87% for the best-performing single-grasp policies, an improvement of 9.97 percentage points. Grasp-TAGS also reduces final orientation errors across all evaluated objects. For more qualitative results, refer to the supplementary webpage: https://rpm-lab-umn.github.io/Grasp-TAGS-web-page/

Abstract
The timeline by which quantum computers can solve breakthrough problems in science depends on improved quantum algorithms as much as on better hardware. Such algorithmic advances are typically achieved through many years of expert human collaboration. In mathematics and computer science, large language models have accelerated progress through automated discovery loops. However, quantum algorithms can only be evaluated by classical computers on restricted or small instances. Here we demonstrate automated discovery of quantum programs beyond these regimes including order-of-magnitude improvements on scientific applications. To achieve this, we build a quantum program library in the Lean theorem prover in which large language model agents synthesize novel programs against formal problem specifications and verify their correctness and cost. Agent teams collaborate, sharing results and knowledge through wikis and discussing ideas at organized conferences. Our results improve on prior baselines for parallel search (up to 1.4x), quantum arithmetic (290x), and simulations relevant to fusion, photochemistry and drug-metabolism by 11x, 8x and 2.5x respectively. Combining human and artificial intelligence at scale, under the rigor of mathematical proof, lets researchers design algorithms that today's classical and quantum computers cannot test, shortening the path to practical quantum computation.

Assessing the Impact of Retrieval-Augmented Generation on Algorithmic Problem-Solving
Authors: Gabriel Souza Baggio, Gabriel Machado Lunardi, Guilherme Medeiros Machado, Subhankar Maity, Faiza Belbachir, José Palazzo Moreira de Oliveira
First: 2026-11-06T17:46:28+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-06T17:46:28+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Competitive programming problems pose distinctive challenges for Large Language Models, as they demand not only proficient code generation but also advanced algorithmic reasoning. This study presents a Retrieval-Augmented Generation approach specifically designed to enhance the capacity of Large Language Models to address such problems. Our approach integrates an embedding-based retrieval mechanism and a similarity search algorithm to identify analogous problems within a knowledge database, employing a dynamically adjusted threshold to mitigate retrieval noise. We conducted an evaluation using the CodeContests dataset, focusing on the test split problems that exhibit similarity to instances from the training split. The experimental framework considered three conditions: (i) problems provided solely with their original descriptions, (ii) problems supplemented with randomly selected examples from the training split, and (iii) problems augmented with similar examples retrieved through the proposed method. In addition, we incorporated a Min-K membership inference analysis to examine whether the generated code solutions were influenced by potential memorization of training data within the evaluated Large Language Models. This complementary perspective enables a deeper understanding of model behavior by distinguishing genuine generalization through retrieval from implicit recall of pretraining samples. Performance was assessed using both pass rates and the CodeForces Elo rating of the problems. The results provide empirical evidence that similarity-based retrieval substantially enhances Large Language Models performance in competitive programming scenarios, offering an scalable framework for improving code generation in algorithmic problem-solving contexts.

Are These Programmers Ripe Enough to Eat? Evaluating How LLMs Handle Lexical Knowledge Conflicts in Translation
Authors: Dongxu Wu, Sara Court, Micha Elsner
First: 2026-11-06T17:44:14+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-06T17:44:14+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
External lexical resources can help large language models (LLMs) translate texts in low-resource languages and specialized domains, but these resources may conflict with models' prior lexical knowledge. To isolate this conflict, we construct a controlled Mandarin-to-English translation task in which familiar source words are assigned new meanings within and across semantic categories. We treat these prompt-supplied meanings as contextual knowledge (CK) and systematically evaluate the effects of word frequency, sentence context, and prompt framing on the translated outputs. Across nine models, CK reliance is greater for less frequent source words and in sentence contexts that support the supplied meanings. Prompt framing can reduce CK reliance, but seven of nine models favor CK even when explicitly instructed to ignore the supplied meanings. Within model families, larger models are more likely than smaller models to utilize CK in their outputs. Three follow-up experiments further show that modal weakening lowers CK reliance, the effect of repetition depends on prompt layout, and the effect of reasoning varies across models. Overall, our controlled study reveals how CK reliance varies across conditions and models, motivating new methods to detect and evaluate lexical conflicts and guide models' lexical choices in LLM-based translation.

Instance-Optimal Estimation with Multiple LLM Judges on a Budget
Authors: Junghyun Lee, Sanghwa Kim, Yassir Jedra, Alexandre Proutière, Se-Young Yun
First: 2026-05-22T08:26:08+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-06T17:36:02+00:00
Comments: 55 pages, 4 figures; the first two authors contributed equally
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Evaluating large language models increasingly relies on LLM-as-a-judge protocols, but such evaluations remain costly: different judges have different prices and reliabilities, and the difficulty of each prompt-response pair can vary substantially. This raises a basic allocation question: under a fixed budget, how should one distribute evaluation queries across heterogeneous judges and instances to obtain the most accurate score estimates? We formalize this question as budgeted heteroskedastic multi-judge estimation under independent, unbiased score observations. Given $K$ prompt-response pairs and $J$ judges with known costs but unknown query-judge variances, our goal is to estimate the bounded score vector while minimizing an $\ell_p$-error. Our first contribution is to analyze the inverse-variance weighted estimator (IVWE) and to derive the oracle allocation that minimizes its leading error bound. Since this allocation depends on the unknown variances, we then address the unknown-variance setting by proposing EST-IVWE, an adaptive algorithm that constructs and uses optimistic variance estimates to stabilize the empirical allocation. We prove that EST-IVWE matches the oracle IVWE rate up to lower-order terms in the budget. Our second and central theoretical contribution is a local minimax lower bound matching the leading allocation-dependent rate, establishing asymptotic instance-optimality up to logarithmic factors. An Assouad-type construction based on local, variance-preserving perturbations yields this bound both in expectation and with constant probability. A complementary Fano-type change-of-measure argument captures the logarithmic dependence on the confidence level with a different (suboptimal) allocation geometry. Finally, we numerically validate our approach on synthetic data and resampled HelpSteer2 evaluations, demonstrating improvements over naïve uniform allocation.

ROSE: Risk-Aware Orthogonal Subspace Navigation for Lifelong Knowledge Editing in Multimodal Large Language Models
Authors: Lingyun Song, Ziyao Chen, Kang Pan, Xiaolin Han, Yifei Zhang, Xiaoqi Wang, Yudai Pan, Xuequn Shang
First: 2026-11-06T17:32:49+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-06T17:32:49+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) require continuous factual updates to stay current, yet lifelong knowledge editing remains a daunting challenge. The primary obstacle is the severe Locality Erosion and Catastrophic Forgetting, where sequential edits inevitably interfere with pre-trained capabilities and prior updates. In this paper, we propose \textbf{ROSE}, a \textbf{R}isk-aware \textbf{O}rthogonal \textbf{S}ubspace navigation framework for lifelong knowledge \textbf{E}diting. ROSE addresses forgetting by aiming to restrict parameter updates to the orthogonal complement of previously edited subspaces. Crucially, it introduces a risk-guided mechanism that utilizes a dynamically computed risk mask to safeguard specific factual parameter subspaces essential for model stability. During inference, ROSE employs a multi-granularity knowledge integration strategy, featuring prototype-anchored semantic gating and perturbation-driven subspace fusion to precisely activate edited knowledge for relevant queries while limiting interference on unrelated inputs. Comprehensive experiments on two prominent multimodal benchmarks show that ROSE is competitive with state-of-the-art approaches across five key metrics, while maintaining outstanding stability over $1,000$ sequential updates. Our code is available at https://github.com/sunlitsong/ROSE.

Learning to Persuade Exposes How Easily LLMs Abandon Correct Beliefs
Authors: Nimet Beyza Bozdag, Emre Can Acikgoz, Gokhan Tur, Dilek Hakkani-Tür

Abstract
Persuasion is a core dynamic of natural language communication, shaping how large language models (LLMs) update beliefs, resolve disagreements, and reach decisions. As LLMs increasingly debate, advise, and think collaboratively with humans and each other, resistance to harmful persuasion becomes a core requirement for reliable behavior. Yet a single targeted persuasive argument can be enough to collapse model accuracy to near zero, even when the argument is factually false. We formalize this threat as adversarial persuasion and introduce an adversarial reinforcement learning framework that trains persuader agents to change a target model's answer in a single interaction. First, we show that RL-trained persuaders raise persuasion success from approximately 24% to over 93% against the training-time persuadee. Second, we find that these learned strategies generalize across four out-of-distribution benchmarks, maintaining 89-95% persuasion success against the training-time persuadee. They also transfer to unseen models, achieving 83% success on Qwen-14B, 79% on Llama-3.1-8B, and 25% on GPT-4o-mini, although effectiveness can be limited for frontier models, with only 4% success on GPT-5-mini. Third, we demonstrate that a curriculum that bootstraps on more persuadable open-weight models before targeting harder models further increases GPT-4o-mini attack success from 25% to 38%. Moreover, our results reveal that optimized persuaders increasingly rely on credibility-based tactics, including fabricated citations and false authoritative evidence. Together, these findings expose a critical weakness in current LLM agents. Even when they initially reason correctly, they can be steered toward false conclusions by optimized natural language influence. This positions persuasion robustness as a necessary safety criterion for multi-agent and human-AI decision-making systems.

Regularized Large Neighborhood Search
Authors: Germain Vivier-Ardisson, Laurent Demonet, Axel Parmentier, Mathieu Blondel
First: 2026-06-01T14:16:18+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-06T17:31:40+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Operations research practitioners typically tackle NP-hard combinatorial problems using large neighborhood search (LNS), a scalable heuristic that iteratively refines a current solution by locally re-optimizing subsets of its variables. In contrast, most existing approaches for integrating combinatorial optimization layers into neural networks still assume access to an exact global solution, which is computationally intractable. We bridge this gap by introducing regularized LNS (RLNS). By regularizing or perturbing local subproblems, we turn the LNS heuristic into an efficient MCMC sampler over the combinatorial set of feasible solutions, with associated Fenchel-Young losses. Under entropic regularization, we prove that RLNS performs exact block Gibbs sampling. Furthermore, adjusting the number of RLNS iterations allows us to interpolate between pseudolikelihood and exact maximum likelihood estimation, for end-to-end learning without global solvers. We demonstrate our approach on $k$-subset selection, generalized assignment, and stochastic vehicle scheduling problems.

Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents coordinate through conversation, and the same message can be phrased in simpler or more complex language, bluntly or politely. We test whether such differences change how other agents judge and act, using the LURK Werewolf framework, which separates each utterance's speech-act predicate from its natural-language realization. We rewrite only the realization, check that the game content is preserved, and replay each game day from saved checkpoints against a re-sampled control. We vary linguistic range consistently across a speaker's messages and politeness strategy per utterance. Across 30 games and 4,335 replays, neither manipulation changes the trust other agents place in the speaker, and no effect on votes or night actions survives correction for multiple comparisons; the largest uncorrected effect is that shifting range away from a neutral paraphrase changes about 4 percentage points more of the other players' votes. In natural play, 88% of face-threatening speech is bald on-record regardless of role.

Abstract

FOSCAR: Accelerating Decode Attention over 2- and 4-Bit KV Caches through Affine Folding
Authors: Donglin Zhuang, Pragaash Ponnusamy, Xiaoxia Wu, Shuaiwen Leon Song, Zhongzhu Zhou
First: 2026-11-06T17:00:25+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-06T17:00:25+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Large-language-model decode attention streams the key-value (KV) cache for every generated token. Packing that cache into 2-bit or 4-bit quantized integers reduces memory traffic but does not automatically translate into latency saving. Existing 2-bit fused kernels reach under 31%/15% of peak HBM throughput on H100/B200. Converting each value and applying its scale and offset before feeding Tensor Cores is a major bottleneck because instruction-issue throughput is scarce on modern GPUs. Instead of applying scale and offset to each KV value, FOSCAR uses affine folding to apply them to the other operand or the output of decode-attention matrix products, depending on the quantization scheme (token-wise or channel-wise). This reduces scale-and-offset operations by up to two orders of magnitude while preserving the dequantized attention output in exact arithmetic. FOSCAR also uses bit manipulation to feed KV operands to Tensor Cores efficiently, requiring only 1 and 0.19 ALU instructions per INT2 element on H100 and B200, respectively. Finally, FOSCAR builds warp-specialized GPU pipelines for Hopper (H100) and Blackwell (B200) using wgmma and tcgen05. On H100, FOSCAR INT2 and INT4 kernels achieve geo-mean speedups of $3.36\times$ and $2.83\times$ over FlashInfer BF16, and $2.03\times$ and $1.71\times$ over TensorRT-LLM FP8. On B200, the corresponding INT2 and INT4 speedups are $2.67\times$ and $2.30\times$ over FlashInfer BF16, $1.48\times$ and $1.28\times$ over TensorRT-LLM FP8, and $1.16\times$ and $1.00\times$ over TensorRT-LLM NVFP4. We integrate FOSCAR into SGLang to benchmark serving efficiency. On long-context serving workloads, FOSCAR increases peak aggregate throughput by up to 170% and 341% over 2-bit and 16-bit baselines, respectively. We open-source FOSCAR as a kernel library at https://github.com/FutureMLS-Lab/FOSCAR.

Large-language-model decode attention streams the key-value (KV) cache for every generated token.
Attend at the Right Resolution: Head-Adaptive Sparse Attention via Query-Key Interaction Structure
Authors: Wajih Hassan Raza, Ahsan Bilal, Yidan Shen, Renjie Hu, Xin Fu, Yu Wen
First: 2026-11-06T16:45:26+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-06T16:45:26+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Attention is a major scalability bottleneck in large language models (LLMs), with its computational cost and key-value (KV) memory traffic becoming increasingly prohibitive as context length grows. Sparse attention addresses this bottleneck by restricting each query to a subset of the context, but its effectiveness hinges on identifying relevant regions both cheaply and reliably. Existing training-free methods primarily rely on runtime relevance estimates, with selection dimensionality and granularity typically governed by fixed heuristics rather than the intrinsic interaction structure of pretrained attention heads. We take a different perspective: the query-key operator of each pretrained head defines a characteristic response structure that determines the resolution needed for sparse selection, both across feature directions and across context tokens. Building on this insight, we propose Head-Adaptive Sparse Attention (HASA), a training-free sparse attention framework that jointly adapts selection resolution along these two dimensions. Along the feature dimension, the singular spectrum of each head's query-key operator determines an effective rank, yielding a compact, head-specific response space. Along the sequence dimension, neighboring regions are progressively merged according to the directional coherence of their token responses in this same space, while heterogeneous regions remain separate. Exact attention is then computed using the original full-dimensional KV states of the selected regions. HASA captures 88.1% of dense-attention mass and, compared with UNIQUE, a strong recent sparse-attention baseline, improves accuracy by up to 3.47 and 2.57 points on RULER and LongBench-Pro, respectively, and accelerates self-attention by up to 1.32x, while achieving up to 14.07x speedup over dense attention.

MemSkill: Learning and Evolving Memory Skills for Self-Evolving Agents
Authors: Haozhen Zhang, Quanyu Long, Jianzhu Bao, Tao Feng, Weizhi Zhang, Haodong Yue, Wenya Wang

First: 2026-02-02T18:53:28+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-06T16:31:39+00:00

Abstract
Most Large Language Model (LLM) agent memory systems rely on a small set of static, hand-designed operations for extracting memory. These fixed procedures hard-code human priors about what to store and how to revise memory, making them rigid under diverse interaction patterns and inefficient on long histories. To this end, we present \textbf{MemSkill}, which reframes these operations as learnable and evolvable memory skills, structured and reusable routines for extracting, consolidating, and pruning information from interaction traces. Inspired by the design philosophy of agent skills, MemSkill employs a \emph{controller} that learns to select a small set of relevant skills, paired with an LLM-based \emph{executor} that produces skill-guided memories. Beyond learning skill selection, MemSkill introduces a \emph{designer} that periodically reviews hard cases where selected skills yield incorrect or incomplete memories, and evolves the skill set by proposing refinements and new skills. Together, MemSkill forms a closed-loop procedure that improves both the skill-selection policy and the skill set itself. Experiments on LoCoMo, LongMemEval, HotpotQA, and ALFWorld demonstrate that MemSkill improves task performance over strong baselines and generalizes well across settings. Further analyses shed light on how skills evolve, offering insights toward more adaptive, self-evolving memory management for LLM agents. Abstract
When researchers refine ideas with large language models over several turns, do the models preserve the original brief's constraints? We introduce DriftBench, a benchmark for constraint adherence in multi-turn LLM-assisted scientific ideation. It pairs 38 research briefs from 24 scientific domains, each with hard constraints and banned moves, with four interaction conditions and seven models from five providers (two open-weight), scored on 2,116 runs. Iterative pressure to improve a proposal raises structural complexity in every model and lowers adherence to the brief's hard constraints. A restatement probe shows that the constraints stay legible and reproducible on demand while the proposal departs from them. The knows-but-violates (KBV) rate, the share of pressure runs scored non-compliant on a hard constraint despite a correct restatement, exceeds 50% for most models. A crossed-judge design measures the judge offset. GPT-5.4 scores adherence 0.535 points lower than Opus 4.6 on the same runs. Re-eliciting the brief at checkpoints lowers KBV for some models but not others, and complexity inflation persists. Blind human raters find more constraint violations than the LLM judge, so reported adherence is conservative. We release all briefs, prompts, rubrics, transcripts, and scores.

A Separation Principle for Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning
Authors: Lucia Pezzetti, Nicolas Lanzetti, Antonio Terpin, Florian Dörfler, Giorgia Ramponi
First: 2026-11-06T15:57:18+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-06T15:57:18+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
We study cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) problems in which agents evolve under decoupled noisy dynamics but share a population objective. A canonical example is a mobility operator that orchestrates a fleet of vehicles to meet customer demand. The stochastic and time-varying nature of these problems calls for learning approaches, yet existing MARL algorithms do not scale: learning assignment and routing jointly over the full state-action space becomes prohibitive as the number of agents grows. Formulating the problem over the space of probability measures, we prove a separation principle: the population cost-to-go function is upper bounded by an optimal transport problem whose transportation cost is the target-conditioned single-agent cost-to-go function. This simplifies the multi-agent problem to learning a single-agent policy and coordinating the agents via optimal transport. Building on this principle, we introduce SALT (Separation-based Assignment and Learning via optimal Transport), a family of algorithms that learn a target-conditioned single-agent policy using any standard reinforcement learning algorithm, and then coordinate the agents via optimal transport. Since learning occurs at the single-agent level, SALT scales to fleet sizes where MARL baselines become intractable and transfers without retraining to fleet sizes unseen during training. In a vehicle-delivery experiment on the southern Manhattan road network, where MARL baselines are computationally prohibitive, SALT improves travel time by 15-21% over a shortest-path routing heuristic. In stochastic grid worlds, SALT reduces average travel times by 23-61% relative to MARL baselines and scales to population sizes where these methods fail.

Know What You Need: Efficient Activation Checkpointing
Authors: Danyal Ahmed, Edouard Oyallon
Venue: The Fortieth Annual Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems, 2026, Paris, France

Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large Language Model training is increasingly constrained by memory capacity. Activation Checkpointing reduces activation memory by avoiding storing selected activations during the forward pass and rematerializing them during backpropagation, thereby trading memory savings for additional computation. Although this technique is essential for scaling model training, rematerialization can be prohibitively expensive, particularly for Transformer linear layers. Approximate computation could reduce this overhead, but conventional approaches either introduce errors that accumulate over the backward pass or fail to exploit information available from the initial forward pass. We introduce a novel approximate rematerialization method that can yield unbiased gradient estimates: using information collected during the initial forward pass, the method selectively recomputes only the most important layer components, either through greedy selection or randomized sketching. Ablation studies show that the resulting gradients closely match the exact gradients. When applied to feed-forward layers, attention layers, or both, our method can recompute only two-thirds of the layer components in a Llama-488M model, while increasing perplexity by just 1.7%, 1.2%, and 2.5%, respectively.

Abstract
Post-training language models for reasoning typically rely on gold answers, task-specific verifiers, or learned reward models, limiting scalability when such signals are scarce. Multi-agent debate offers a self-supervised alternative, but converting debate into reliable training supervision remains challenging: majority voting weights agreeing responses equally, lacks a principled tie-breaking rule, and is ill-defined for open-ended tasks without a canonical answer; likelihood-based scoring can conflate fluency with correctness; and homogeneous committees may reinforce correlated errors. We introduce Alignment by Confident Debate (AbCD), which converts debate traces into preference data without gold answer labels. A sampling-diverse committee debates each problem, after which final answers are clustered by task-level semantic equivalence. We use verifiable tasks (mathematical reasoning, multiple-choice quantitative QA, and executable code-generation) as a controlled test bed: their answer keys or executable checkers let us evaluate the quality of debate-derived supervision against ground truth, even though the oracle itself never uses gold labels. Post-training on the resulting preferences outperforms inference-only ensembling and debate, as well as SFT on the same traces, across five model families spanning 1B to 8B parameters and four datasets, improving consensus accuracy, with a maximum gain of $21.5_{pp}$. Ablations show that the approach transfers to a larger model and to code generation, while also revealing limitations of heterogeneous committees and agreement-based weighting. Overall, the results suggest that debate traces can yield useful preference supervision when answer equivalence is combined with confidence-weighted disagreement.

Abstract
Large-scale foundation models exhibit behavioral shifts when subjected to interventions such as scaling, fine-tuning, reinforcement learning with human feedback, or in-context learning. Current explainability methods are structurally ill-suited to explain these shifts, because they either treat models as static objects, as traditional eXplainable AI (XAI) approaches do, or merely compare independent explanations across different checkpoints of a model. As a result, these approaches fail to explain the functional transition between two model instances in which a certain behavior has shifted following an intervention. This gap creates significant governance risks across jurisdictions including the EU AI Act, US state legislation, and Chinese AI regulations, which require documenting causal chains for substantial system modifications. This position paper argues that explaining behavioral shifts in large language models requires a principled approach that treats the shift itself as the primary object of explanation: namely, one that explains how and why an intervention transforms a reference model into an updated model with different behavior. To support this claim, we introduce Comparative XAI (XAI$_Δ$), a novel XAI paradigm aimed at explaining the difference between two model checkpoints where a behavior has shifted, together with a set of desiderata specifying what XAI$_Δ$ explainers and explanations must satisfy, including comparability, validity, actionability, and monitoring, with the goal of grounding model auditing in explicit, measurable requirements. Finally, we provide preliminary evidence suggesting the need for XAI$_Δ$ in practice through illustrative experiments, compiling the resulting findings into a transition report directly usable for governance and incident documentation.

Abstract
Traditional information theory provides powerful tools for analyzing statistical properties of information but offers limited insight into meaning and semantic transformation. It has never been possible to quantify the meaning of an arbitrary piece of information. Large language models represent information as vectors embedded within high-dimensional semantic spaces, enabling meaning to be analyzed geometrically. This paper defines semantic state as the vector representation of information within a model's semantic space and defines cognitive work as the transformation between semantic states, movement through semantic space. To quantify this transformation, we introduce the Contextual Information Differential (CID), a covariance-aware metric based on Mahalanobis distance measuring semantic displacement relative to the structure of the embedding manifold. By treating meaning as a geometric property of semantic space, CID provides a practical method for measuring semantic change, cognitive transformation, and information processing. This framework establishes a foundation for analyzing cognitive processing, learning, reasoning, knowledge generation, synthetic expertise, and human/AI collaboration through implementation-independent measurement of semantic state change in both human and artificial systems.

Abstract
It has long been proposed that animals construct cognitive maps to support flexible behavior in novel environments. We tested whether large language models form analogous internal representations using relational reasoning tasks and causal "mosaic" experiments. Models beyond the ten-billion-parameter scale inferred unstated relationships and generalized to synthetic entity names. Abstract
Reinforcement learning with rule-based, binary-valued reward functions has been shown to significantly enhance the reasoning capabilities of large language models. Abstract

Abstract
