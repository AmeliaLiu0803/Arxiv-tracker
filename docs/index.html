Discovering state-of-the-art quantum circuits with large language models
Authors: Andi Gu, Alek H. Kemeny, Susanne F. Yelin, Mikhail D. Lukin
First: 2026-10-15T17:59:20+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-15T17:59:20+00:00
Comments: 46 pages, 10 figures, 6 tables
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Significant efforts are currently being directed toward the implementation of large-scale quantum information processors, but realizing useful quantum advantage also requires the development of efficient quantum algorithms. While for many practical problems the dominant resource costs are associated with a small set of computational primitives, optimizing them in the quantum setting presents unique challenges, including a reversibility constraint absent from ordinary classical computation. Here we introduce an evolutionary framework in which large language models (LLMs) propose circuit designs and an independent evaluator verifies and scores them. We apply this framework to tasks spanning numerical computation, chemistry, optimization, quantum simulation, and number theory. In seven of the eight tasks, the discovered circuits significantly reduce both Toffoli and qubit counts relative to the state of the art. In the eighth, the search discovers a new reversible architecture for prime-field modular inversion. By handling reversibility differently, the architecture makes divsteps -- a classical algorithm previously impractical for reversible integer arithmetic -- viable and substantially improves the Toffoli-qubit trade-off for modular inversion. These results open the door for evaluator-guided LLM co-design of new algorithmic structures for quantum computation.

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are expanding from assisting scientific research and engineering to autonomously developing and improving AI systems, advancing AI for AI (AI4AI) as a pathway toward recursive self-improvement (RSI). Workflow-driven machine learning engineering (MLE) systems provide a practical path toward this pursuit through complex orchestration that manages long-horizon experimentation, while recent MLE systems adopt model-directed interaction, allowing models to autonomously organize experiments and adapt optimization strategies based on execution feedback. However, feedback from each experiment reflects several preceding decisions, and a long MLE run contains many such costly experiments. Training must therefore select useful supervision from long interaction trajectories and learn from intermediate outcomes. We propose a post-training method for autonomous long-horizon MLE optimization and apply it to train MLExpert, a 35B Mixture-of-Experts agentic model. We design the Kaggle-MLE Factory to construct executable tasks and an interaction harness for model-directed experimentation. Hierarchical agentic SFT transfers general tool-use and coding capabilities to MLE through outcome-guided supervision. Windowed Interaction RL (WIRL) treats the multi-turn decisions between successive experiment results as an execution window and assigns it a shared outcome-based reward. Asynchronous collection and policy correction support policy updates before the trajectory ends. On MLE-Bench Lite, MLExpert improves the average medal rate of the base model from 37.9% to 72.7%, achieving performance comparable to larger frontier models such as Kimi-K3 and GLM-5.3. Further experiments show that MLExpert improves performance on NatureBench Lite, agent scaffold optimization, and algorithm optimization, suggesting that the learned optimization capability generalizes to other tasks.

Abstract
We introduce 3DCodeVerse, a research ecosystem that enables coding agents to create editable and verifiable 3D content at scale. 3DCodeVerse comprises large-scale, high-quality datasets of executable 3D code, a coding harness for spatial reasoning and automated verification, and practical guidance for 3D coding. First, we construct 3DCodeVerse-2M, a large-scale curated dataset of standalone 3D executable code. We build this corpus by collecting open-source data, converting specialized representations, and generating new programs with coding agents. Each program is packaged with the source files and associated assets required for execution in its declared environment, along with available descriptions and verification records. Second, we develop the 3DCodeVerse Harness, a multi-agent directed acyclic graph framework for generating and refining 3D content. It uses execution checks, spatial measurements, and visual feedback to automatically evaluate and revise generated programs without human feedback during generation. Third, we conduct systematic empirical studies of 3D coding, establishing baselines with fine-tuned open models and deriving practical guidance through component-level analyses of our harness. Extensive experiments demonstrate that fine-tuning on our dataset significantly improves the 3D coding capabilities of open-source models. Notably, our 3DCodeVerse harness achieves a 90% win rate against Claude Design (3D Object) in pairwise human evaluations, when both are utilized by Claude Opus 5.5.

Evaluation Awareness Beyond English: Do LLMs Act Differently When Tested in Indic Languages?

First: 2026-10-15T17:53:33+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-15T17:53:33+00:00

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) can often tell when they are being tested. This ability, known as evaluation awareness, can lead models to act more cautiously during safety evaluations than in real use. Existing work has studied evaluation awareness almost entirely in English, although models serve users in many languages. Indic languages are spoken by over a billion people, yet evaluation awareness remains almost entirely unstudied in them. We test four instruction-tuned models from three families in five Indic languages alongside English. A set of cues varies whether the model is told that it is monitored and whether the monitoring is a safety test. Models refuse harmful requests more often when they are told that they are being tested. This increase appears in all four models and all six languages. In Indic languages, refusal of harmful requests rises by 14.4 percentage points on average. The test cue also brings Indic refusal of harmful requests level with English, but raises refusal of benign Indic requests from 6.9% to 11.3%. We release all 47,880 judged responses to support further work. These results suggest that safety evaluations in Indic languages may not reflect how models treat their everyday users.

Designing Transformers for Continuous Diffusion Language Models
Authors: Weijian Luo, Yujian Chen, Hao Jiang, Guang Cheng, Tianyang Hu
First: 2026-10-15T17:52:31+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-15T17:52:31+00:00

Abstract
Continuous diffusion language models (CDLMs) offer an alternative approach to parallel language generation; however, most existing studies directly use transformer architectures from either causal large language models (LLMs) or Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) for image generation, which were not optimized for CDLMs. In this paper, we conduct a systematic study on Transformer architectures for CDLMs and introduce Language Diffusion Transformer (LangDiT), combining feature and query-key normalization, wider noise conditioning, likelihood-shortcut gradient routing and calibration, and confidence-gated training self-conditioning. LangDiT significantly reduces generation perplexity in matched LangFlow comparisons, establishing a new state of the art in continuous language generation across different model formulations. Using LangFlow as a representative CDLM, LangDiT as a plug-and-play backbone leads to a new state-of-the-art model with a generation perplexity (GenPPL) of 21.72 using 1024 NFEs and 44.72 GenPPL using 128 NFEs on the unconditional generation of 1024-token sequences on the OpenWebText dataset. When adapted to Plaid-style CDLM formulations, LangDiT results in a new leading GenPPL of 11.33 using 1024 denoising NFEs, and 27.51 GenPPL using 128 denoising NFEs. These gains are achieved while retaining each model's underlying representation, training objective, and inference sampler, isolating transformer architecture as a fundamental source of improvement. Our study identifies architectural design as an important source of improvement in continuous diffusion language models.

Learning Self-Orchestration in Decentralized Agent Swarms
Authors: Guowei Xu, Zhenting Qi, Amani Kiruga, Himabindu Lakkaraju, Yilun Du

Abstract
The capabilities of an agent swarm depend on both its individual members and how they coordinate. Yet existing multi-agent systems often train agents within fixed orchestration structures, separating the learning of individual capabilities from the organization of collective behavior. We introduce market-based post-training (MPT), a framework for jointly learning action policies and self-orchestration in decentralized agent swarms. Agents express their willingness to participate through bids, which determine who acts and how collective outcomes provide local training credit. Learning both action and bidding behavior allows individual capabilities and patterns of participation to adapt together without a central coordinating agent. We evaluate MPT on embodied crafting, robot navigation, and deep research. Results show gains over monolithic post-training and confirm that agent participation indeed evolves with learning. With the same post-trained action policies, learned self-orchestration outperforms alternative coordination strategies. Ablations show that both action and bidding benefit from learning, and that learned bids provide more effective action-training signals. Beyond the original training objectives, trained swarms adapt faster to related tasks and achieve better performance with larger populations at inference time. Larger populations also suffer smaller performance losses under agent dropout, demonstrating that learned self-orchestration supports reuse, scaling, and robustness in decentralized agent swarms. Code and trained models are available at https://github.com/Embodied-Minds-Lab/MPT.

A team of Gemini 4 Argon agents beats Minecraft
Authors: Maxence Faldor, Satinder Singh, Roberta Raileanu

Abstract
We placed four Gemini 4 Argon agents in a sandboxed Minecraft survival world and gave them one goal: defeat the Ender Dragon. Each agent controlled a player via an open-source library called Mineflayer and coordinated with its teammates through in-game chat and shared memory. No one assigned roles or wrote game logic. The agents planned, wrote, and debugged every program themselves. Researchers could watch the run on a live dashboard and pause or stop it at any time, but they had no cause to intervene. Starting from a randomly generated world on Easy difficulty, the team wrote 2,783 programs and defeated the dragon in 27 hours and 14 minutes, dying 605 times along the way. To our knowledge, this is the first end-to-end autonomous completion of Minecraft by AI agents, achieved using only the standard Mineflayer library without pre-scouted seeds or other game-specific tools written by humans. Winning required the agents to keep a coherent plan across thousands of dependent steps for more than a day. Complex real-world software engineering and enterprise tasks demand the same long-horizon coding and multi-agent coordination. We describe the setup, how the run unfolded, the team's strategies (dividing the work, sharing what they learned, recovering each other's items), and the limits of the result.

Abstract
Large language model (LLM)-based agents and multi-agent systems increasingly tackle complex tasks through long-horizon tool use and inter-agent collaboration. However, these systems are prone to deviating from the overall goal as execution unfolds, which wastes execution resources and leads to undesired outcomes, a failure termed goal drift. In this work, we propose a steering agent that supervises execution on the fly and steers the system back on track when drift occurs. The steering agent observes the execution context, deciding when to intervene with a low-cost fast path and how to intervene with a high-cost slow path. To unlock its steering intelligence, the ability to assess goal drift and generate effective interventions, we jointly train the two paths with reinforcement learning, combining a pairwise ranking objective with group-relative policy optimization (GRPO). Experiments on information-seeking search tasks show that the steering agent provides effective supervision for both single-agent and multi-agent systems, improving their performance and efficiency. The learned steering intelligence generalizes to out-of-domain tasks, transfers to unseen executors, and supports controllable intervention frequency without retraining.

Abstract
Multi-agent LLM systems that generate structured workflows from natural-language requests are now deployed in production across cloud automation, DevOps, and enterprise orchestration. Operating them exposes a recurring change-management problem. Routine updates, such as re-running an input, swapping the LLM, or refactoring an agent's prompt or orchestration code, often produce workflows that differ substantially from validated references. Engineers then lack a principled way to decide whether a change is safe to ship. Automatic workflow evaluation is the natural tool, but in practice metric scores are poorly calibrated, and a numeric change rarely communicates the severity of the underlying degradation. We introduce WorkflowPerturb, a controlled benchmark that applies realistic, graded perturbations to golden workflows. It contains 4,973 golden workflows and 44,757 perturbed variants across three perturbation types (Missing Steps, Compressed Steps, Description Changes) at severities of 10%, 30%, and 50%. We benchmark multiple metric families, analyzing their sensitivity and calibration using expected score trajectories and instance-level alert rates. Our results characterize systematic differences across families and support severity-aware interpretation of workflow evaluation scores in change-management settings. WorkflowPerturb is publicly available at https://huggingface.co/datasets/microsoft/WorkflowPerturb .

Abstract
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) enhances the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) by querying external structured knowledge. However, it can also introduce privacy risks by leaking sensitive information from the retrieval datastore. We propose a simple method to preserve datastore privacy in RAG systems via random projection. By applying the same projection to both datastore embeddings and query embeddings, our method provably preserves semantic similarity between queries and retrieved items while substantially mitigating data extraction attacks. Across multiple RAG architectures and datasets, we show that this lightweight approach achieves superior retrieval and generation performance compared to prior methods which leverage formal differential privacy (DP) guarantees, while exhibiting comparable empirical privacy under strong attack models. Our results for the first time suggest that random projection can serve as a competitive and practical baseline for privacy-preserving RAG systems.

Towards Continual Learning: Mitigating Forgetting With Subspace Tuning
Authors: Arnav Chavan, Kusha Sareen, Rishabh Tiwari, Abhranil Chandra, Johan Obando-Ceron, Kurt Keutzer, Scott Niekum, Inderjit S Dhillon, Devvrit Khatri
First: 2026-10-15T17:44:43+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-15T17:44:43+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) acquire new skills through fine-tuning, but it can destroy capabilities they already have: catastrophic forgetting remains a central obstacle to continual learning in LLMs. Recent works connect forgetting to how far, and along which directions, fine-tuning moves the weights. On-policy methods such as reinforcement learning and on-policy distillation avoid updating the principal singular directions of weight matrices and thus forget less. In contrast, SFT concentrates updates along these directions and is susceptible to catastrophic forgetting. In this work, we introduce Subspace Tuning (SST), which restricts each weight update to the bottom singular subspace, eliminating changes along the principal directions. SST is optimizer-agnostic and composes well with fine-tuning recipes including SFT and self-distillation. SST combines tight subspace protection, dense gradient estimation, and a simple refresh mechanism, enabling stronger learning than prior orthogonal fine-tuning methods. With these mechanisms, protecting as much as 99\% of the weight subspace substantially improves retention while preserving strong target-task performance. This behavior strengthens with model and data size and nearly eliminates forgetting in the large-model regime. SST also keeps the model close to its base distribution, inducing $2$--$4\times$ less KL drift than the baseline recipes. Overall, SST advances the learning--forgetting Pareto frontier, making it a practical default for fine-tuning pre-trained language models.

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are becoming prevalent in scenarios where honesty is critical. However, despite alignment training, these models still exhibit dishonest behaviour, such as outputting claims that contradict some knowledge or are not supported by what is accessible in context. We introduce Continuous Adversarial Training for Consistent Honesty (CATCH), inspired by a method originally developed to robustly refuse harmful queries. Learning Rational Strategies for Temporal Objectives in Multi-Agent Systems
Authors: Omar Adalat, Edwin Hamel-De le Court, Francesco Belardinelli
First: 2026-10-15T17:42:52+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-15T17:42:52+00:00

Abstract
We study rational synthesis for stochastic multi-agent systems with unknown dynamics, where each agent is equipped with its own probabilistic temporal objective specified using Probabilistic Linear Temporal Logic on Finite Traces ($\mathsf{PLTL}_f}$). We impose solution concepts (e.g., Nash equilibrium, subgame-perfect equilibrium) as constraints on the joint strategy profile, using payoffs derived from each agent's probability of satisfying its temporal objective. In particular, we formulate per-agent rational synthesis as finding a strategy profile -- if one exists -- such that (i) the designated agent's probabilistic temporal constraint is satisfied, and (ii) the profile represents a stable solution under a chosen solution concept. We give conditional convergence guarantees for tabular Nash-Q learning, certify satisfaction with simultaneous finite-sample confidence bounds, and assess strategic stability with approximate best-response checking. Our approach operates in a stochastic \emph{Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning} (MARL) environment, where model-free learning algorithms attempt to attain extremal satisfaction probabilities for temporal objectives automatically compiled into reward automata. Our approach is empirically validated using 8 general-sum concurrent stochastic games.

Abstract
When robots are deployed in the real-world, they must interact with other agents and accomplish their own goals while adhering to legal constraints. These potentially conflicting objectives are often only partially prioritized, making scalarized objectives or fully specified hierarchies unsuitable. We present a framework for multi-agent motion planning under legal requirements based on games with partially ordered preferences (posetal games). Scenario-independent legal guidelines order abstract legal concerns, from which we automatically derive agent preferences and a legal game for any concrete scenario. Agents may refine their legal preferences with private objectives, and regulators may refine the guideline as laws evolve. We prove that such customizations can only remove equilibria and thus preserve legality. To coordinate agents without explicitly communicating private preferences, we introduce belief play, which provably recovers the true equilibria from conservative local games, and a distributed iterative best-response protocol requiring less information. We apply the framework to maritime navigation, systematically lifting pairwise traffic rules to multi-vessel encounters. On seven encounters with up to four vessels, every rule violation of the committed equilibria is certified as forced by the legal hierarchy or unsatisfiable, and defectors are identified from public information in all tested cases. These results indicate that guideline-based posetal games offer an auditable route from partially specified laws to multi-agent motion planning.

Abstract
Agents are increasingly interacting with other agents that act for different principals (people or organizations on whose behalf they work), such as a buyer's personal agent negotiating with an enterprise's seller agent. In such agent-to-agent (A2A) scenarios, each agent has its own environment and holds information and authority that the other cannot see. Moreover, the interests of each agent may be aligned (cooperative), partly aligned (mixed-motive), or opposed (competitive). Existing multi-agent system failure studies assume one orchestrator, shared workspace, or single principal. We ask how A2A systems fail when these assumptions are removed. We study more than 2,500 recorded runs of A2A systems built from different agent harnesses and models, spanning 488 scenarios across eight families (legal, CRM, security, commerce, etc.). From these traces, we derive $A^2$-Failures, a taxonomy of 20 A2A failure modes in four categories: (i) discovery and delegation, (ii) grounding and record consistency, (iii) verification, and (iv) conduct and fidelity. Three findings hold across every harness, model, and scenario studied: (1) discovery and interaction are driven by the phrasing of the task rather than the availability of peer agents; (2) no agent verifies a peer's factual claim on its own initiative, so an A2A system inherits the reliability of its least reliable participant unless the protocol or mechanism performs the check; and (3) the aggregate outcome score credits the agent completing the task while staying blind to the coordination, verification, and conduct failures beneath it, so A2A systems must be evaluated per agent, against each agent's own principal. We release the run logs behind the taxonomy at https://huggingface.co/datasets/a2a-salesforce/a2-failures.

Abstract
Materials and chemical optimization must choose the next synthesis, characterization, or high-fidelity simulation from sequential observations under a limited budget. Large language models can use task knowledge to propose adjustments, but composing surrogates, acquisition functions, and search strategies is constrained by compatibility, parameter dependencies, and evidence requirements. We present Agentic-MatriBO, a capability-compiled agentic Bayesian optimization (BO) framework that represents BO components and selection conditions as composable capabilities. A language policy proposes BO recipes and candidate plans; a compiler checks proposals against the registered capability catalog; and a deterministic execution layer validates and commits candidates. Oracle observations are stored in research memory and inform later rounds. We compare Agentic-MatriBO with eight baselines on four offline finite candidate pools---COF, PCE10, Buchwald--Hartwig (BH), and Suzuki--Miyaura---using five seeds per condition; results are reported descriptively. Across the four tasks, Agentic-MatriBO has the best mean endpoint quality after orienting each objective so that higher is better. Its normalized area under the best-so-far quality curve during the BO stage (nAUC-BO) is substantially higher on COF, near parity with the best baseline on PCE10 and BH, and lower on Suzuki. COF ablations show that removing the capability compiler or evidence gate coincides with lower cumulative quality and fewer exact-optimum hits. The framework provides an inspectable interface for evidence-conditioned BO adaptation.

Abstract
Reasoning over long document contexts remains a challenge for large language models (LLMs). Standard retrieval methods are inherently reactive, bottlenecking agents with complex, cross-document reasoning at inference time, while precomputed structures like knowledge graphs often fall short because they rely on rigid heuristics. Instead, can we allow agents to perform anticipatory directed curation: learning to proactively reorganize unstructured corpora prior to inference to efficiently serve diverse future queries. We build on a key asymmetry in modern agentic harnesses (e.g., Claude Code): while LLM agents are adept at navigating existing filesystems to solve an immediate task, they are rarely trained to construct reusable workspaces for future use. To instantiate this capability, we introduce Librarian Agents, a framework in which a builder agent transforms raw documents into a hierarchical filesystem of synthetic files and reference links, which forms a workspace tailored for a downstream answering agent. We formulate workspace construction as a Markov decision process and optimize the builder policy via reinforcement learning to directly maximize downstream answering accuracy. Empirically, Librarian Agents outperforms the strongest retrieval and precomputation baselines by 33 percentage points on BrowseComp-Plus, amortizes upfront construction costs across repeated queries, and generalizes zero-shot to unseen domains on BRIGHT with a 31-point average accuracy improvement.

Abstract
Watermarking provides a principled way to authenticate text generated by large language models (LLMs). In practice, however, the final text may be mixed-source, with watermark evidence surviving at only a subset of token positions after rewriting, insertion, deletion, or paraphrasing. Although prior work has studied global detection of watermark signals, when such signals can be localized remains unclear. We formulate watermark localization as a token-level multiple-testing problem based on pivotal statistics, with a latent indicator recording whether watermark dependence survives at each position. Under an asymptotic regime indexed by exponents for signal sparsity, next-token concentration, and effective-vocabulary growth, we derive a sharp boundary for global detection and phase transitions for discovery and classification within the class of coordinatewise pivot-based localization rules. We show that discovery is strictly harder than detection and that consistent classification is impossible across the parameter regime within this class. We then develop an adaptive thresholding method that does not require knowledge of the exponents or time-varying next-token distributions, but uses a data-driven estimate of the surviving watermark fraction. The method attains the optimal discovery boundary and near-optimal discovery power relative to homogeneous pivot-based rules. Simulations support the theoretical phase transitions, while experiments on model-generated texts demonstrate practical localization performance under common edit mechanisms.

Abstract
Reinforcement learning has become a widely used approach to post-training large language models. In a typical implementation, learning is off-policy: The policy generating the responses differs from the policy being trained because of training-inference mismatch or policy staleness in asynchronous training. This discrepancy is commonly addressed by importance sampling, whose ratio is usually regularized in practice through truncation, masking, or clipping. Under regularized importance sampling, the importance-weighted policy gradient is no longer invariant to constant shifts in the reward. This invariance is recovered by using the covariance between the reward and the importance-weighted score as the estimand, which coincides with the policy gradient when the ratio is not regularized. This note develops this covariance perspective for both sequence-level and token-level importance sampling. The covariance perspective reveals the roles of the group-mean baseline and score centering in policy gradient estimation. Both perform centering in covariance estimation, but they differ in the mean being subtracted: The group-mean baseline uses a sample mean, whereas score centering uses a population mean. The two lead to distinct unbiased covariance estimators with different variances. Whether both forms of centering should be used simultaneously depends on whether importance sampling is applied at the sequence level or at the token level. With sequence-level importance sampling, score centering adds no benefit once the group-mean baseline is used; with token-level importance sampling, using the group-mean baseline without score centering generally introduces bias when the importance ratio is regularized.

Reinforcement learning has become a widely used approach to post-training large language models.

Abstract
Reducing memory use in large language model (LLM) training is an ongoing challenge. Activation checkpointing saves memory by recomputation, while lossy compression changes the stored activations. The reemergence of sparse $\relu$ and $\relu^2$ activations creates a third opportunity: losslessly compressing the activation cache itself. We present BitSparse, a GPU-resident lossless compression format for sparse activations. Compression and reconstruction run in custom Triton kernels fused into an autograd function, shrinking the activation cache while recovering it exactly for the backward pass. Bfloat16 (BF16) and FP8 activations are supported. Optional sign-bit packing further increases compression up to close to the theoretical limit. We analyze its compression ratio and compare it to the theoretical optimum. BitSparse lowers per-token VRAM by 31\% and 30\% Modded-NanoGPT and Nemotron-H respectively, with overheads of no more than 1\% at practical sequence lengths. Additionally, a new preallocated buffer system avoids host synchronization during training when variable size tensors are allocated.

Abstract
Though large language models (LLMs) have enabled great success across a wide variety of tasks, they still appear to fall short of one of the loftier goals of artificial intelligence research: creating an artificial system that can adapt its behavior to radically new contexts upon deployment. One important step towards this goal is to create systems that can induce rich representations of data that are seen in-context, and then flexibly deploy these representations to accomplish goals. Recently, Park et al. (2024) demonstrated that current LLMs are indeed capable of inducing such representation from context (i.e., in-context representation learning). The present study investigates whether LLMs can use these representations to complete simple downstream tasks. We first assess whether open-weights LLMs can use in-context representations for next-token prediction, and then probe models using a novel task, adaptive world modeling. Furthermore, we assess closed-source, state-of-the-art reasoning models on the adaptive world modeling task, demonstrating that even the most performant LLMs cannot reliably leverage novel patterns presented in-context. Overall, this work seeks to inspire novel methods for encouraging models to not only encode information presented in-context, but to do so in a manner that supports flexible deployment of this information.

Abstract
A new adaptive restart rule for Genetic Algorithms (GAs) is considered. The rule is based on the Schnabel Census method, originally developed for statistical estimation of a size of animal population. In this paper, the Schnabel Census method is applied as a heuristic to estimate the number of different solutions that may be visited with positive probability, given the current distribution of offspring. The new restart rule also tends to be superior to the well-known rule, which restarts an algorithm when the current iteration number is twice the number of iterations till the current best incumbent was found.

Abstract

Abstract

Abstract
LLM systems increasingly rely on groups of agents that discuss, critique, and check one another, assuming that peers will catch individual errors. We test this assumption against a minimal adversary. In a ten-agent factual discussion, one or three adversarial agents repeatedly assert a plausible false answer. After each round we probe each benign agent's private answer through a separate call whose response is hidden from its peers, and we separately label its public stance toward the claim.
