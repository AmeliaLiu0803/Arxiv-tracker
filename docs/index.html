World of SWECraft: Agent Collaboration at Scale via Simulated Software Worlds
Authors: Shannon Zejiang Shen, Yapei Chang, Qiyao Liang, Benjamin Newman, Jenny Liang, Rulin Shao, Ziqiao Ma, Junjie Oscar Yin, Yung-Sung Chuang, Weijia Shi, Yejin Choi, Luke Zettlemoyer, Pang Wei Koh, Mohit Iyyer, Tongshuang Wu, David Sontag

Comments: 33 pages, 9 figures, 7 tables
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
As teams of AI agents take on larger projects and long-standing open problems, agent collaboration offers another dimension for scaling inference-time compute. To understand what makes such collaboration effective and scalable, we develop Software World, a framework for simulating multi-agent coworking in realistic software ecosystems. It comes with a pipeline to create environments of real, interdependent Python packages of different topics, sizes, and dependency structures, which we call software worlds. In a simulation, agents maintain packages in the world with a high-level goal of improving software efficiency, and they are free to discover tasks to work on and collaborate with others. We evaluate agents' work in a world collectively by measuring speedup in hidden dependent packages. The framework allows us to study different elements of collaboration by varying communication, ownership, and resource allocation. We run simulations in three different worlds, totaling more than 10,000 hours of agent work across eight models. Overall, performance improvement scales with inference-time compute, with the best model improving downstream efficiency by up to 31%. We show that most agents can find meaningful tasks via collaboration, but some frontier models can prematurely stop looking for improvements and make little progress. We release the simulation framework and traces to support future studies of agent collaboration at https://szj.io/sw.

LongZero: Enhancing Long-Context Reasoning in LLMs from Zero Data
Authors: Zhennan Wan, Jianfei Chen
First: 2026-10-21T17:59:14+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T17:59:14+00:00
Comments: Code and data are available at https://github.com/zhennan1/LongZero
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) is an effective way to strengthen the long-context reasoning of large language models (LLMs), but it needs training data that are long, diverse, challenging, and verifiable. Human annotation of such data is expensive, naturally occurring data are too scarce to sustain RL, and existing synthesis methods depend on external corpora or stronger teacher models, which become harder to obtain as the trained models get stronger. Recently, RL from zero data, in which a model learns from tasks it proposes itself, has achieved excellent performance, but existing methods target short-context tasks. Therefore, we propose $\textbf{LongZero}$, a method that lets an LLM improve its own long-context reasoning without any external data. Instead of writing questions and answers over existing documents, LongZero first has the model synthesize the question and answer with its supporting evidence in a compact structured form, and only then separately expands the evidence into a long multi-document context. LongZero combines these self-written multi-hop reasoning tasks with self-written multi-round retrieval tasks and trains the model with GRPO. We evaluate LongZero on two LLMs across five long-context reasoning benchmarks and compare it with RL on data synthesized from external corpora. Experimental results show that LongZero can generalize to real tasks, and outperforms both baselines, suggesting a viable and promising path to improving long-context reasoning of LLMs.

Quant-Arena: Benchmarking LLM Agents for Quantitative Trading Research

Abstract
Quantitative research is an iterative loop: a researcher proposes a market hypothesis, implements it as an executable signal, tests it on historical data, and decides what to keep. Existing benchmarks score formulas, code, or individual trading decisions, leaving open whether large language models (LLMs) can complete this loop and produce strategies that work beyond the research period. We introduce Quant-Arena, a benchmark and public leaderboard for end-to-end quantitative research on real A-share market data. A model constructs features and stock-scoring code, receives feedback only on 2022--2023, and freezes one strategy for assessment on the sealed year 2024. A cash-account evaluator applies trading constraints and costs, including market impact estimated from a simulated order book. Scores are dominated by realized excess return and complemented by signal quality and the diversity of features used by the strategy. We evaluate nine frontier models, among which GPT-6 Astra scores highest; yet no model beats the equal-weight market in 2024. Quant-Arena is open to submissions from any model or agent harness.

Differentially Private Sparse Reward Estimation with Preference Feedback
Authors: Meng Ding, Mingxi Lei, Jie Zhang, Jinyan Liu, Di Wang
First: 2026-10-21T17:45:42+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T17:45:42+00:00
Comments: 36 pages
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) has become a central paradigm for aligning Large Language Models with human values. However, the reliance on sensitive preference data necessitates rigorous differential privacy (DP) guarantees to prevent user information leakage. Existing private alignment methods typically assume dense reward parameterizations, resulting in estimation error bounds that scale polynomially with the ambient {model size $d$}. Such dependence is prohibitive in modern {overparameterized models} where $d$ is vast. In this work, we study \emph{Private Sparse RLHF} problem to address this limitation, assuming that human preferences (reward model) are governed by a subset of $s^* \ll d$ relevant features. We provide the first comprehensive theoretical framework for differentially private sparse reward estimation from pairwise preference feedback under the Bradley-Terry-Luce model, and introduce efficient algorithms for both Sample-DP and Label-DP settings. Theoretically, under the squared $\ell_2$ parameter estimation error, we establish upper bounds of $\widetilde{O}(s^*/n + {s^*}^2/n^2\varepsilon^2)$ for $(\varepsilon,δ)$-Sample-DP and $\widetilde{O}(s^*/n + s^*/n\varepsilon^2)$ for $\varepsilon$-Label-DP based on randomized response, where $n$ is the training data size. For the lower bounds, we show that the Sample-DP rate is nearly optimal, and that the guarantee for our Label-DP method cannot be further improved within the randomized-response framework. We also provide a general lower bound for the problem with Label-DP. Empirically, our framework demonstrates superior efficacy over dense baselines across sentiment generation and safety alignment benchmarks, offering a significantly more favorable privacy-utility trade-off.

Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) has become a central paradigm for aligning Large Language Models with human values.
Learning Large-Scale Competitive Team Behaviors with Mean-Field Interactions
Authors: Bhavini Jeloka, Yue Guan, Panagiotis Tsiotras
Venue: NeurIPS 2026
First: 2025-04-29T20:31:59+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T17:35:53+00:00
Comments: Accepted at NeurIPS 2026
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
While multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) has been proven effective across both collaborative and competitive tasks, existing algorithms often struggle to scale to large populations of agents. Recent advancements in mean-field (MF) theory provide scalable solutions by approximating population interactions as a continuum, yet most existing frameworks focus exclusively on either fully cooperative or purely competitive settings. To bridge this gap, we introduce MF-MAPPO, a mean-field extension of PPO designed for zero-sum team games that integrate intra-team cooperation with inter-team competition. MF-MAPPO employs a shared actor and a minimally informed critic per team and is trained directly on finite-population simulators, thereby enabling deployment to realistic scenarios with thousands of agents. We further show that MF-MAPPO naturally extends to partially observable settings through a simple gradient-regularized training scheme. Our evaluation utilizes large-scale benchmark scenarios using our own testing simulation platform for MF team games (MFEnv), including offense-defense battlefield tasks as well as variants of population-based rock-paper-scissors games that admit analytical solutions, for benchmarking. Across these benchmarks, MF-MAPPO outperforms existing methods and exhibits complex, heterogeneous behaviors, demonstrating the effectiveness of combining mean-field theory and MARL techniques at scale.

Does Centralized Learning Help Embodied Agents Coordinate, and When Can a Tabular Study Tell?
Authors: Muhammad Ahmed Atif, Nehal Naeem Haji, Muhammad Ebad Atif, Mohammad Shahid Shaikh
First: 2026-01-24T13:09:01+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T17:09:52+00:00
Comments: 10 pages, 2 figures. Under peer review at AAMAS 2027
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Tabular studies are a common way to compare independent and centralized multi-agent learners, because they avoid function approximation. A natural expectation is that centralized learning, which learns joint actions, helps agents coordinate, especially when their bodies make solo success hard. Earlier work tests this only in parts: it measures coordination for one learning method, compares methods with the agents' bodies fixed, or varies speed but reports only how well the team did. We test it in a predator-prey environment, where teammates cooperatively hunt together while two teams compete. We compare independent Q-learning (IQL) with central Q-learning (CQL) in all four predator and prey pairings, under limits on speed and stamina, on a 5x5 and an 8x8 grid trained for 200,000 episodes each, and check that the agents learn on both. We measure coordination directly, with predictions for the main measurement fixed in advance. In this task, where one predator can capture prey alone, we find three things. First, CQL costs its agents in both roles on both grids, and its predators coordinate no more than IQL's. Second, its one benefit comes from acting on the best joint action: this cut capture time by 42% on the small grid with faster prey and unlimited stamina, but helped nowhere on the larger grid, where the best joint action had mostly never been tried. Third, on the larger grid a tie-breaking default decided which method won, even though the agents had passed a learning check. A tabular comparison can therefore be trusted only if it also checks how much of the state space the agents covered and implements the methods alike. We propose simple checks for both.

Tabular studies are a common way to compare independent and centralized multi-agent learners, because they avoid function approximation.

Abstract
Robots must act under physical and social constraints while adapting to changes in their environment and human needs. Existing embodied-agent systems often focus on specified goals or abstract execution into symbolic actions, limiting evaluation in an evolving world. We introduce TARSphere, a robot-oriented full-stack framework for physically and socially grounded long-horizon embodied agents. TARSphere-Infra runs robot policies in a physics simulator with embodied NPCs and constrained dialogue. An event-driven finite-state machine (FSM) uses completed actions and delivered information to update task states and evaluate outcomes. TARSphere-Harness, its embodied brain harness, lets a frozen multimodal large language model (MLLM) act through multi-level executable skills, using observations, hierarchical memory, and execution feedback to revise its decisions. Our evaluation of 8 MLLMs shows that general-purpose models can outperform embodied models, while task updates and human interaction remain difficult. Frequent collisions and falls further motivate evaluating execution safety alongside task success, even with a shared action interface and decision budget.

Abstract
Leading commercial endpoint detection and response (EDR) products have shifted from operator-configured rule sets to multi-component systems where autonomous AI components operate alongside, and increasingly in place of, operator-deployed policies. Autonomous defense agents using commercial EDR as their hardening tool are no longer tuning a passive tool, but a black-box autonomous system capable of making vendor-specific decisions. We present the first evaluation framework for autonomous defense agents hardening commercial EDR. We instantiate it in a Game of Active Directory (GOAD) lab with Horizon3's NodeZero as the autonomous pentester and Microsoft Defender XDR as the primary EDR under test. We extend the same framework to CrowdStrike Falcon and SentinelOne Singularity to test whether our Microsoft Defender XDR findings hold across vendors. We run a sample benchmark of defense agents with two large language model (LLM) backbones (Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Cisco Foundation-Sec-8B). We report three lessons learned that neither simulation nor open source EDR evaluation can surface: (i) commercial EDR telemetry is engineered for Security Operations Center (SOC) analyst workflows rather than for measuring policy effectiveness; (ii) per-policy attribution is important for separating the defense agent's actions from the EDR's autonomous actions; and (iii) EDR behavior varies between runs, largely because containment varies and accumulates. Together, these findings highlight a sim-to-real gap for enterprise defense and motivate evaluation methodology for benchmarking autonomous defense agents in environments with black-box, autonomous tools.

Abstract
Understanding large, complex, and rapidly evolving code bases remains a core challenge for software engineering at scale, hindering onboarding, maintenance, refactoring, and impact analysis. Providing this information to a Large-Language Model (LLM) is another hurdle, as the LLM must understand not only the required code method but also its associated methods in the grand scheme of the repository. LLM-as-a-judge evaluation using the popular RAGAS of the Code Comprehensor output yields above 90% faithfulness scores.

EPIC: Rethinking Trust Regions for Autoregressive LLM Policies via Exact-KL Prefix Importance-Ratio Clipping
Authors: Chengxi Li, Hanlin Wu, Zhicheng Cai, Huan-ang Gao, Wei-Ying Ma, Jingjing Liu, Hao Zhou
First: 2026-10-21T16:37:09+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T16:37:09+00:00
Comments: 27 pages. Code: https://github.com/LiChengxi666/EPIC
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) based on PPO-style clipping has become an effective approach to improving the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). However, standard token-level clipping measures policy change only through the conditional probability of the current token, while the prefix conditioning context is itself generated by the policy. This mismatch becomes especially consequential in long-response and off-policy training. We reformulate autoregressive generation as a finite-horizon Markov decision process and derive its performance-difference identity under behavior-policy sampling. The resulting change of measure identifies the cumulative likelihood ratio of the generated prefix as the exact importance weight for each stepwise policy-improvement term. Starting from the monotonic-improvement principle of trust-region policy optimization, we then construct a prefix-level trust region using the exact reverse-KL coordinate $r-1-\log r$, whose behavior-policy expectation equals the corresponding prefix-distribution KL divergence. This yields EPIC, a PPO-style sequence-clipping method that directly regulates prefix-level policy drift without relying on a local quadratic approximation or expert-routing replay. Experiments on off-policy reinforcement learning with a sparse mixture-of-experts language model show that EPIC consistently outperforms standard and sequence-level clipping baselines across mathematical-reasoning benchmarks, while remaining competitive with routing-replay-based alternatives. Code is available at https://github.com/LiChengxi666/EPIC.

DialFork: Going Beyond Factuality to Evaluate Procedural Hallucination in Conversational Agents
Authors: Sergio Burdisso, Esaú Villatoro-Tello, Dairazalia Sánchez-Cortés, Yanis Labrak, Jordi Luque, Milos Cernak, Petr Motlicek
First: 2026-10-15T09:18:32+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T16:28:11+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) deployed as conversational agents are increasingly expected to follow procedural logic. While factual hallucination has received extensive attention, a different failure mode has been overlooked: procedural hallucination, in which an agent produces individually fluent and factually plausible utterances while deviating from the intended conversational process. We introduce DialFork, the first benchmark to measure this failure mode on raw dialogue text. DialFork is built by forking conformant dialogues at controlled turn positions and injecting one of four hallucination types, yielding 36,278 dialogues across 24 tasks in 12 domains; 30 human annotators validate the construction. We define three evaluation tasks that characterize procedural deviation at three granularities: how far a set of dialogues deviates from conformant ones (monitoring deployed agents), how far a single dialogue does (procedural retrieval), and at which turn a dialogue first goes off-track (real-time guardrails). For the first two, we propose dialog-flow-aware metrics, FlowKL and FlowScore, that compare dialogues by how they unfold rather than their content. At the dialogue level, FlowScore significantly outperforms all baselines across seven encoders, showing that semantic similarity is no substitute for procedural similarity. We release DialFork under open licenses at https://github.com/idiap/dialfork, hoping the community will measure conversational process alongside content.

Authors: Jiayi Chen, Hui Li, Yujiong Shen, Qiyuan Peng, Yanxin Li, Chenhao Huang, Shaofan Liu, Guoqiang Zhang, Junjie Ye, Shihan Dou, Ming Zhang, Maxm Pan, Tao Gui, Qi Zhang, Xuanjing Huang

Abstract
Benchmark performance is the dominant standard for comparing large language models (LLMs), typically summarized by a single aggregate score, such as accuracy, pass rate, or rubric score. This aggregate score provides limited information about a model's underlying capabilities, yet understanding these capabilities is crucial for model development and evaluation. To this end, we propose Tree-Induced Diagnostic Evaluation (TIDE), which recasts diagnostic evaluation as behavior-driven decision-tree induction to perform fine-grained capability diagnosis. TIDE recursively builds a hierarchical capability tree from benchmark samples and model scores: at each node, an LLM judge proposes candidate splits and performs sample-to-group classification, and behavior-based information gain selects among them. We apply TIDE to five models across four benchmarks to demonstrate its effectiveness, and find fine-grained capability contrasts invisible at the aggregate level: Claude Opus 4.6 achieves 100% on microeconomic market reasoning where GPT-5.4 scores 83.3%, but trails GPT-5.4 on contract law reasoning with 77.8% versus 88.9%. We hope TIDE can be extended to past and future benchmarks so that the rich behavioral signal in evaluation results is fully leveraged for understanding model capabilities.

Abstract
Given the enormous number of claims to be checked, verifiable legal AI requires scalable, cost-effective models that answer the practical needs of law. System One decision models are built on large language models but, instead of generating text, return a calibrated probability for each option. This suits the verification of legal authority, where the question is closed: a given holding, or none of them. We test whether such models abstain when the correct authority is missing. We extend CaseHOLD with an abstention condition in which the correct holding is replaced in 1,670 of 5,314 items, and compare four decision models with six generative LLMs. When no option is correct, generative LLMs still pick one in 38-86% of items; the most cautious decision model does so in 30%. On standard CaseHOLD, a decision model matches the best generative LLM (88.1% vs. 88.2%) at 1/55 of the cost. Accepting that LLM's answer only when a decision model agrees halves its wrong citations, and distilling a decision model's probabilities into Qwen3-8B, without labels, yields a well-calibrated local model. Legal AI should be judged by its wrong citations, not by accuracy alone.

ToM2Vec: Continuous Theory-of-Mind Tokens for Multi-Agent Coordination
Authors: Nithin Sivakumaran, Ali Payani, Hyunji Lee, Mohit Bansal, Elias Stengel-Eskin

Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Forming an accurate theory-of-mind (ToM) is key to successful multi-agent collaboration and requires reasoning under uncertainty to capture multiple possible partner states. This is hard to do in the text representation space native to large language model (LLM) agents, which more easily describe a single partner state. To address this challenge, we introduce ToM2Vec, which learns to form abstract theories-of-mind in a rich latent space via a fixed budget of continuous reasoning tokens. During training, supervision for these tokens comes from the partner's actual thoughts, which the ToM tokens are trained to abstract and match. At test-time, ToM tokens are generated by each agent and act as conditioners for that agent's thoughts and actions. Across several environments covering collaborative, competitive, and mixed-motive settings (Overcooked-AI, multiparty negotiation, Decrypto, Hanabi, and Collab Escape/Capture), we evaluate ToM2Vec's ability to support multi-agent interactions and collaboration, finding consistent improvements over the base model and a variety of competitive trained and prompted baselines. We show that latent reasoning is critical, with performance dropping when reasoning is expressed in text. Moreover, we show that ToM2Vec training generalizes to partner model families not seen at train-time, including a proprietary model (GPT-5.4). In our analysis, we provide evidence that the latent tokens encode a ToM, rather than task-specific information: ToM2Vec's tokens improve the agent's ability to directly answer questions about partner beliefs, desires, and next actions.

Abstract
As large language models (LLMs) continue to grow in scope and capability, privacy concerns are on the rise. Since users increasingly interact with a single general-purpose system across many distinct contexts, there is a natural tendency to pursue a one-size-fits-all solution to privacy. Asking a Multimodal LLM Whether Someone Fell: Zero-Shot Screening of Low-Resolution Thermal Video in a Laboratory and an Occupied Home
Authors: Marcos Junca, Mauro Junca
First: 2026-10-21T15:53:17+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T15:53:17+00:00
Comments: 13 pages, 6 figures. Data: https://senecta.ai/research/thermal-fall-llm-data.zip
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Thermal cameras can watch over older people living alone without capturing faces, and multimodal large language models (LLMs) can judge a short video without task-specific training. We evaluate a production multimodal LLM, Gemini 3.8 Flash at temperature 0, as the decision stage of an event-triggered fall screener, used exactly as a deployed prototype uses it: one request per short thermal clip, with its sound track when there is one, and its predecessor Gemini 3.7 Flash on the same requests. On 438 staged falls and 563 non-fall windows that we located by hand in a public 32x24 thermal dataset, the deployed request called 69% of the held-out subjects' falls (67% over all subjects; bed falls excluded) and none of 499 non-fall windows, including people lying on the floor and getting up. Over 76 h of ordinary life in an occupied room, recorded at 160x120 with sound, it called none of 395 clips a fall (95% upper bound 1.2 false alarms per day) and caught 4 of the 9 staged falls the camera could see. One cautionary sentence in the prompt ("a person who goes out of view ... is not a fall") accounted for many of the misses: removing it raised sensitivity on the held-out subjects from 69% to 83% on 3.8 Flash and from 80% to 92% on Gemini 3.7 Flash, and in the home from 4 to 9 of the 9 visible falls, with no false call in ordinary living but at the price of calling deliberate lie-downs a fall. Sensitivity collapsed at 8x6 pixels (34%); identical requests disagreed on 4% of windows; and when the model called a fall, its explanation placed it within a second of the annotated landing 99% of the time. Two conventional detectors trained on the laboratory data caught nearly every held-out laboratory fall but raised 14.2 and 23.9 false alarms per day in the home.
