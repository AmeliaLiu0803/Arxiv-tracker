DEAF: A Benchmark for Diagnostic Evaluation of Acoustic Faithfulness in Audio Language Models
Authors: Jiaqi Xiong, Yunjia Qi, Qi Cao, Yu Zheng, Yutong Zhang, Ziteng Wang, Ruofan Liao, Weisheng Xu, Sichen Liu
First: 2026-03-17T15:52:26+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-09T17:56:35+00:00
Comments: 14 pages,6 figures
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Recent Audio Multimodal Large Language Models (Audio MLLMs) demonstrate impressive performance on speech benchmarks, yet it remains unclear whether these models genuinely process acoustic signals or rely on text-based semantic inference. To systematically study this question, we introduce DEAF (Diagnostic Evaluation of Acoustic Faithfulness), a benchmark of over 2,700 conflict stimuli spanning three acoustic dimensions: emotional prosody, background sounds, and speaker identity. Then, we design a controlled multi-level evaluation framework that progressively increases textual influence, ranging from semantic conflicts in the content to misleading prompts and their combination, allowing us to disentangle content-driven bias from prompt-induced sycophancy. We further introduce diagnostic metrics to quantify model reliance on textual cues over acoustic signals. Our evaluation of seven Audio MLLMs reveals a consistent pattern of text dominance: models are sensitive to acoustic variations, yet predictions are predominantly driven by textual inputs, revealing a gap between high performance on standard speech benchmarks and genuine acoustic understanding.

Abstract
In medical education, physicians convert academic knowledge into clinical expertise through residency: years of training across thousands of encounters, with diverse sources of feedback and progressively greater autonomy. Much of clinical reasoning relies on the patient encounter, a dialogue in which a clinician elicits history, refines diagnostic hypotheses, and decides management under uncertainty. While large language models (LLMs) excel on static medical benchmarks, methods to optimize the full sequence of clinical decisions remain underdeveloped. We present ResidencyRL, a reinforcement learning (RL) method for training clinical artificial intelligence (AI) agents through simulated multi-turn clinical encounters (up to 60 dialogue turns and 8 tool calls per trajectory). ResidencyRL pairs the policy agent with LLM simulators capable of complex, adversarial behaviors, training against a structured reward aligned to diagnostic accuracy, management quality, communication, documentation, and safety. On held-out evaluations, the ResidencyRL agent improves diagnostic accuracy by 7.0% under adversarial conditions (88.0% vs. 81.0%) and reduces missed red flag rates by 31%, demonstrating rigorous mitigation of premature closure. Blinded expert clinicians validated these gains, preferring the trained agent in 87.6% of side-by-side comparisons. The procedural competencies transfer to unseen benchmarks: the agent outperforms the base model across all six clinical axes of the AMIE multi-visit benchmark, and shows consistent directional improvements on AgentClinic and CRAFT-MD. Our findings demonstrate that sequential clinical decision-making can be effectively learned through multi-turn RL in simulation, yielding robust, generalizable capabilities, paving the way towards clinical mastery. Prospective validation with real-world workflows remains necessary to establish clinical utility.

Task Context Shapes Component Roles in Language Models
Authors: Sovesh Mohapatra, Dani S Bassett
First: 2026-10-09T17:40:53+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-09T17:40:53+00:00
Comments: 9 pages, 5 Figs
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language models reuse the same attention heads across tasks, but identifying these shared heads does not explain what they do differently in each task. A natural source of inspiration is task-dependent interactions among shared brain regions, which motivate a network view of language-model computation. Here, we compare fixed model components across tasks using identical graph facts, statements specifying which directed connections are present. We measure how interventions affect attention heads and the activation state in later layers (influence), as well as the answer (importance). Task changes redistribute the influence in five open-weight models spanning four families and 1.5-14 billion parameters. In Gemma-2-9B and Qwen3-14B, an activation edit changes an early internal activation vector and switches which fact the model reads while the written instruction stays fixed. The same heads then respond to different graph facts and influence later components differently. For each prompt, we construct two linear response maps, one before the edit and one after it. Each map predicts a later head's response from a measured change in an earlier head. For a separate factual intervention, the map for the corresponding condition reduces mean squared prediction error by 53.6% in Gemma and 5.0% in Qwen compared with the average of the two maps. These findings show that task context changes both which graph facts the shared components respond to and how their outputs affect later computation. A network account may help explain how models use shared parameters to perform different computations. Measurements of task-dependent influence could also guide interventions intended to alter one behavior while preserving others.

The Illusion of AI Expertise Under Uncertainty: A Probabilistic Paradigm to Interpret Results Under Ground Truth Variation
Authors: Aparna Elangovan, Lei Xu, Mahsa Elyasi, Ismail Akdulum, Mehmet Aksakal, Enes Gurun, Brian Hur, Saab Mansour, Ravid Shwartz Ziv, Karin Verspoor, Dan Roth
First: 2026-01-09T03:19:37+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-09T17:38:00+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Benchmarking the capabilities of AI systems, including Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision Models, typically ignores the impact of uncertainty in the underlying ground truth answers from experts. This ambiguity is not just limited to human preferences, but is also consequential even in safety critical domains such as medicine where uncertainty is pervasive. In this paper, we introduce a probabilistic paradigm to theoretically explain how high certainty in ground truth answers is almost always necessary for even an expert to achieve high scores. In contrast, in datasets with high variation in ground truth answers, the expert performance drops substantially resulting in almost no difference between a random labeller and an expert. This characteristic also manifests when comparing models, where uncertainty obfuscates differences between poor and high performing models. Therefore, ignoring uncertainty in ground truth evaluation data can result in the misleading conclusion that a non-expert has similar performance to that of an expert. Given ground truth answer variability and commonly used F1 and accuracy metrics, we bring forth a simple and explainable probabilistic coin toss model to estimate expected accuracyand expected F1 and compare the estimated scores with those that experts (human or model) achieve across multiple domains -- including radiology and models such as GPT-5 and Gemini-3. The results lead to the recommendation that stratification by the probability of the ground truth answer becomes critical when expert performance is relatively low. Under stratified evaluation, performance comparison becomes more reliable in high certainty bins, mitigating the effect of the key confounding factor --- uncertainty.

Same Query, Different Answer: Run-to-Run Variance in Semantic Query Processing
Authors: Oktie Hassanzadeh, Dharmashankar Subramanian
First: 2026-10-09T16:34:13+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-09T16:34:13+00:00
Comments: Code and data: https://github.com/semviews/sqp-variance-study
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Semantic query engines ask a large language model (LLM) to judge individual rows, and benchmarks compare these engines on the quality of a few executions at temperature 0. We measure how much another execution changes. We re-execute the text and tabular workloads of SemBench, LRO-Bench, and UDA-Bench through their own harnesses, log every one of 1.2 million model calls, and reanalyze SemBench's published executions. At temperature 0, between 0.07% and 10.2% of rows change their operator output over five executions, depending on the model, and up to 24.0% on one task. Plans amplify these changes exponentially in the number of rows an answer exposes, as a possible-worlds model predicts: on UDA-Bench, a few percent of extracted values change, but about two thirds of the answers do. In SemBench's published results, 21% of text results change quality between executions, including on engines whose plans are deterministic. Several per-query system comparisons, and 16 of 28 tests of LRO-Bench's implementation recommendations, are not settled by the executions the benchmarks run. Changing rows are rows on which models disagree with each other; identical requests within one execution disagree as often as across executions; and one execution's decision margin or reasoning length predicts which rows change. Voting reduces the variance but does not remove it, and a calibrated cascade keeps its guarantee while the rows it returns change. We derive reporting rules for benchmarks and design rules for engines.

QTestBench: A Test Framework for Evaluating LLM-Generated Quantum Code
Authors: Meriem Terki, Nouhaila Innan, Minghao Shao, Muhammad Kashif, Alberto Marchisio, Muhammad Shafique
First: 2026-10-09T16:23:46+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-09T16:23:46+00:00
Comments: Accepted at the 2026 IEEE 2nd International Conference on Quantum Artificial Intelligence (QAI), 2026
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have shown strong results in classical code generation and are increasingly used to write the unit tests that safeguard that code. Whether they can do the same for quantum software is less clear, because quantum programs are probabilistic, depend on numerical tolerances, and are judged by domain-specific checkers, so a well-formed test can still disagree with the benchmark's verdict. Existing quantum benchmarks measure whether a model produces a correct solution, but not whether its tests can distinguish correct implementations from faulty ones. We introduce QTestBench, a framework that evaluates LLM-generated quantum test suites against independent benchmark checkers, never treating the tests themselves as ground truth. For each task, QTestBench prompts models to produce syntactic, semantic, and behavioral tests, injects candidate solutions at runtime, and compares each test verdict with the checker's label under three validation cohorts, each answering a distinct diagnostic question. We benchmark five LLMs on eight PennyLane QHack challenges and ten Qiskit HumanEval tasks. Generated suites almost always execute, but discrimination lags behind: the best generator reaches a test quality of 0.71 on QHack and 0.34 on Qiskit HumanEval, so executability alone overstates test quality. Oracle-verified synthetic mutants stabilize bug-detection estimates when natural faulty solutions are scarce, as on three of ten Qiskit tasks that yield none. QTestBench offers a reproducible, cross-framework benchmark for the trustworthiness of LLM-generated quantum tests.

Abstract
Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) has become a prominent research direction, particularly within collective robotics, where the ARGoS simulator is widely used to study emergent swarm behavior. Yet despite this growth, systematic support for reuse and reproducibility in MARL-based swarm robotics remains limited and insufficiently standardized: a single experiment is assembled from manually aligned simulator files, training scripts, configuration values, paths, seeds, and environment details, so reuse and reproduction stay fragile even within one research group. A five-dimension evaluation (architecture, efficiency, reproducibility, usability, and collaboration) supports our claim that managing experiment variation as a platform responsibility makes ARGoS+MARL experiments easier to reuse, reproduce, and hand off. Abstract
In this work, we present the \texttt{LLM ORDER BY} semantic operator as a logical abstraction and conduct a systematic study of its physical implementations. First, we propose several improvements to existing semantic sorting algorithms and introduce a semantic-aware external merge sort algorithm. Our extensive evaluation reveals that no single implementation offers universal optimality on all datasets. From our evaluations, we observe a general scaling relationship between sorting cost and the ordering quality for comparison-based algorithms. Building on these insights, we design a budget-aware optimizer that utilizes heuristic rules, LLM-as-Judge evaluation, and consensus aggregation to dynamically select the near-optimal access path for LLM ORDER BY. In our extensive evaluations, our optimizer consistently achieves ranking accuracy on par with or superior to the best static methods across all benchmarks. We believe that this work provides foundational insights into the principled optimization of semantic operators essential for building robust, large-scale LLM-powered analytic systems.

Learning to Report Unsafe Tasks in a Multi-Agent Game
Authors: Avyay M. Casheekar, Hariganesh Tangirala
First: 2026-10-06T18:57:07+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-09T15:34:05+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
When a team of agents shares a reward for completed tasks, reporting and stopping unsafe work reduces the team's reward. Random audits that penalize silence can make universal reporting optimal without ensuring that a silent team learns to report. We study this problem in a game where any witness can stop a task by reporting. When a task's k witnesses share a policy and draw actions independently, the derivative of expected reward with respect to the shared silence probability at universal silence counts its benefit k times, while the comparison between universal reporting and silence counts it once. A fully shared policy can therefore be locally attracted to silence even when reporting is optimal. For any grouping into shared policies, we give an audit condition sufficient for exact policy-gradient updates to reach universal reporting and, outside boundary cases, necessary from near universal silence. In a balanced family, meeting the condition with prescribed margins costs k times as much for a fully shared policy as for one policy per role. We test these predictions with PPO on 24 witness graphs, starting from learned silence. Witness groups, which place co-witnesses in separate networks, reduce unsafe task completion by 33.59 percentage points relative to shuffled groups of the same sizes under the same audits (95% graph-bootstrap interval: 21.03-45.13). Only 9 of 48 witness-group runs achieve below 1% unsafe completion while retaining at least 90% legitimate completion. At the same audit budget, a fully shared network meets both thresholds in 0/48 runs with independent action draws and 48/48 with a common draw. On 24 further graphs with witness groups fixed, audits allocated by the condition reduce unsafe completion by 42.22 points relative to audits maximizing a static coalition deterrence ratio at the same budget.

SafeDiSTL: Decomposing Joint Constraints for Safe Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning
Authors: Hongrui Zheng, Aditya Singh, Rahul Mangharam

Abstract
Cooperative multi-agent tasks often impose constraints on a shared objective or collective safety. A team level violation cost function can measure this constraint outcome, but does not distinguish which agents' actions most strongly influence that violation. We present SafeDiSTL, a constrained multi-agent reinforcement learning framework that allows for credit assignment even with joint constraint measurements. We use Signal Temporal Logic (STL) robustness to measure episode-level violation through running-maximum cost increments. Smooth full-episode robustness and differentiable dynamics provide action sensitivities, which allocate each cost increment across agents at the same decision time. These sensitivities account for delayed effects when weighting agents' shares of each cost increment. We study the framework in cooperative payload transport, connected navigation, and networked traffic control tasks. SafeDiSTL achieves 1.6-5.1x the safe-success rate in transport, improves navigation safe-success by 11.1%, and delivers 50-68% more throughput in traffic when compared to constrained baselines. Results demonstrate a strong improvement of the learned policy in dealing with shared constraint while retaining the performance of unconstrained policies.

MAGE: Multi-Agent Self-Evolution with Co-Evolutionary Knowledge Graphs
Authors: Ruiyi Yang, Zechen Li, Hao Xue, Imran Razzak, Flora D. Salim
First: 2026-05-11T06:39:51+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-09T15:19:08+00:00

Abstract
Self-evolving language-model agents must decide what to learn next and how to preserve what they have learned across iterations. Existing systems typically carry this cross-iteration knowledge as natural-language feedback, flat episodic memory, or implicit reinforcement signals, none of which cleanly supports a frozen weak backbone at inference time. This paper introduces MAGE (Multi-Agent Graph-guided Evolution), a framework that externalizes self-knowledge into a four-subgraph co-evolutionary knowledge graph. Its experience subgraph stores both teacher-written failure corrections and the learner's own past correct reasoning traces, which are retrieved as task-conditioned guidance for a frozen execution model. During evolution, the graph, a task-level search bandit, and a skill-level routing bandit are updated from the same reward stream, while the learner's backbone remains unchanged. We further provide structural analysis showing how append-only memory growth, bounded curriculum coverage, and task-filtered retrieval together support stable improvement of the retrieval substrate for frozen-learner evolution. Across nine benchmarks spanning mathematical reasoning, multi-hop and open-domain question answering, spatio-temporal analysis, financial numerical reasoning, medical multiple-choice, an open-world survival game, and web navigation, MAGE achieves strong performance against prompt-based frozen-backbone baselines. Ablations show that self-harvested success traces and teacher-written corrections are complementary, with success memories contributing most on reasoning-template-heavy tasks and corrective memories supporting harder composition and interaction settings. Code is available in https://github.com/RuiyiYang01/mage-alg

OpenSocialJax: Evaluating Social Adaptation in Sequential Social Dilemmas with Open-Ended Environmental Dynamics
Authors: Zihao Guo, Meng Fang, Joel Z. Leibo, Yali Du
First: 2026-10-09T15:04:28+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-09T15:04:28+00:00

Abstract
Sequential social dilemma benchmarks capture diverse social interactions, but rarely test whether agents can infer hidden environmental rules through repeated interaction while adapting to other agents. We introduce \textbf{OpenSocialJax}, which extends Clean Up and Commons Harvest across an open-ended distribution of latent environment configurations, systematically varying spatial layouts and hidden rules governing tool crafting, payoffs, and resource dynamics. Each episode samples one configuration from tens of millions of possibilities and holds it fixed across five trials. We evaluate eight large language models, including three closed source models and five open source models. Our evaluation tests whether models can discover hidden rules and adapt to other agents in homogeneous and mixed teams. We find that rule discovery often improves performance but does not reliably sustain cooperation, as the cleaning burden becomes concentrated among fewer agents in OpenCleanup and every team eventually exhausts the shared resource in OpenHarvest. In our mixed arena, one cooperative agent was able to sustain the public good in OpenCleanup at a cost to its own return, but could not prevent resource depletion in OpenHarvest. Together, these results highlight the difficulty of adapting jointly to unfamiliar environmental dynamics and the changing behavior of other agents.

Inject a Turn, Not a Token: Learning Where to Redirect LLM Reasoning for Self-Correction
Authors: Jiwon Jeon, Myungsik Cho, Jeonghye Kim, Youngchul Sung

Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language models struggle to reliably identify and correct errors in their own reasoning. Unlike prior training-based approaches that fine-tune an LLM to generate revisions or feedback, we learn where to intervene in failed rollouts and determine how to redirect them. To choose where to intervene, we train a 130K-parameter MLP head on a frozen encoder using only final-answer correctness. For redirection, we find that under a fixed generation budget, the tested inline injections fail to reliably redirect reasoning, whereas presenting the same instruction in a new user turn changes the model's course. The resulting correction framework, UPTURN (User-Prompt TURN injection at a learned position), requires no LLM fine-tuning: it retains a selected prefix, injects an instruction as a new user turn, and regenerates the suffix. Across four reasoners and six math benchmarks, UPTURN improves average accuracy in every setting over a 32-rollout baseline. It outperforms self-correction and reflection-token injection baselines under matched correction budgets, transfers across reasoners and to GPQA-Diamond without retraining, and complements GRPO and self-correction methods.

Abstract
Many machine learning approaches are two-level: an expert selects a class of methods, within this class the choice of key components is critical and usually manual. This paper addresses the automatic tuning of these components and proposes a framework transferable across models of different nature. The multi-agent large language model (LLM) framework has three roles---analyst, coder, and recorder~---with an experiment log and a sandboxed execution environment. Unlike systems that optimize the entire ML script or search architectures in a discrete space, the framework operates over an open but semantically narrow space: the agent generates code only for the specified components, leaving the overall network structure unchanged. The framework thus tunes key components on tasks of different nature without changing its internal structure.

Abstract

Abstract
Tables are ubiquitous across diverse domains, yet reasoning over them remains a significant challenge for modern large language models (LLMs). Current approaches typically linearize tables into sequences, inherently overlooking their intrinsic two-dimensional and hierarchical structure. To address this, we propose H2Table (Hierarchical Hypergraph-Enhanced Table Reasoning), a novel framework that represents complex tables as hierarchical nested hypergraphs. To process this representation, we design a tailored hypergraph encoder to facilitate message passing between hyperedges (headers) and nodes (cells), thereby perceiving the semantic entailment relationships between them within complex tables. Experimental results demonstrate that our approach effectively handles complex table question answering tasks with hierarchical nested headers. Notably, on the HiTab dataset, H2Table achieves an average improvement of 22.88% over state-of-the-art baselines on highly complex tables with a nesting depth of four. Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used throughout the software development lifecycle. Deceptive behavior takes the form of misleading responses or assistance in fabricating, concealing, or distorting information. To study this behavior, we introduce SWE-DeceptionBench, a benchmark for evaluating LLM deception in software engineering. The benchmark covers six lifecycle stages and two deception triggers: Active Deception, where models recognize infeasible or conflicting requirements but still present them as achievable, and Passive Deception, where models help fulfill explicit user requests to mislead others. We further introduce Neutral, Reward, and Pressure conditions to study the effect of external inducements, resulting in 720 test instances in Chinese and English. Evaluating seven mainstream LLMs, we find substantial deception even under the Neutral condition, with aggregate rates of 49.88% for Active Deception and 64.29% for Passive Deception. Deception rates vary across lifecycle stages, and external inducements affect Active and Passive Deception to different extents. We also evaluate lightweight defenses, finding that guard models miss many deceptive requests, and Self-Reminder reduces but does not eliminate deceptive responses. These findings highlight the need for more effective deception detection and mitigation methods to support trustworthy LLM-based software engineering.

Abstract
Feature selection is a central problem in high-dimensional financial regression, where large numbers of engineered variables and strong correlations among predictors reduce model stability and interpretability. $L_1$ regularization (Lasso) is widely used for sparse regression, but its shrinkage can be restrictive when informative signals are spread across correlated variables. Exact sparse regression avoids this shrinkage but requires solving a combinatorial optimization problem that is computationally difficult and is typically fit to minimize in-sample rather than out-of-sample error. This paper presents a feature selection framework based on Monte Carlo Tree Search with Generalized Nested Rollout Policy Adaptation (GNRPA). Feature selection is formulated as a sequential decision process, and candidate subsets are scored by their out-of-sample validation performance rather than training error. The method also supports a lexicographic objective accounting for subset size and multicollinearity. We study this approach on a contemporaneous explanatory regression task: explaining the weekly return of the NASDAQ-100 index from 1,072 engineered features spanning market, macroeconomic, and factor data. On this task, the proposed method identifies sparse feature subsets with higher out-of-sample explanatory power than an optimized Lasso baseline, reaching an out-of-sample $R^2$ of 0.6961 versus 0.6313 for Lasso. These results suggest that Monte Carlo Tree Search is a practical alternative to convex regularization for sparse explanatory feature selection in financial regression, with potential value for factor attribution and risk decomposition where transparency matters as much as out-of-sample fit.

Abstract
We present Rationally Linearized Multilayer Perceptrons(RaLiMPs), a new approach to efficiently linearizing MLPs using low-degree rational approximations of standard activation functions. Inspired by polynomial-based MLP linearization, we show that rational approximations can provide accurate representations at substantially lower degree. Abstract
Near-term quantum approaches to combinatorial optimization are limited by qubit counts, circuit fidelity, sampling cost, and the difficulty of encoding constraints, while machine learning is increasingly used to configure and control quantum optimization workflows. We call such workflows adaptive: decisions conventionally fixed in advance, from formulation and penalties to shot budgets, backends, and whether to invoke a quantum processor at all, are made by learned policies that respond to the instance, the progress of the solve, or the hardware. This review examines three paradigms, AI for quantum optimization, quantum for AI-driven optimization, and AI-quantum co-optimization, and organizes the literature by the decision being learned rather than by application. A structured search identified a 119-paper corpus, of which 67 papers form an evidence core coded in detail. The evidence is considerably stronger for AI-assisted quantum optimization than for the reverse direction: learning already reduces quantum evaluations, improves initialization, supports decomposition and penalty control, and mitigates noise, whereas evidence that quantum computation improves learned optimizers remains largely confined to small-scale simulation. Experimental controls are thin: of 52 studies in the three paradigms, 22 include no classical baseline, the quantum contribution is fully isolated in 10 of 24 where an ablation applies, and the median largest experiment uses 16 qubits. We introduce an M0-M5 evidence hierarchy, from simulation to practical quantum advantage, and find no broadly convincing result at the highest level. We argue that scaling is increasingly a systems problem: the question is not only whether a problem fits on a quantum processor, but how classical and quantum resources should be allocated across the optimization process.

Abstract
Collective Autonomous Vehicle (AV) route choices in real-world traffic networks can create externalities for Human-Driven Vehicles (HDVs), as routes that benefit AVs may increase travel times for other road users. To address this, we introduce a social component, the marginal cost, to the selfish travel time AV reward. Marginal cost captures an AV's impact on other road users by comparing their travel times with those in a counterfactual simulation in which the AV is removed. We study AV route choice in mixed-autonomy traffic settings using Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL), where AVs learn routing policies through repeated interactions with the traffic environment. Experiments on the real-world networks of Ingolstadt and Provins show that incorporating marginal cost into the AV reward not only limits the increase in HDV travel times and shortens AV travel times relative to selfish AV routing, but also improves overall system performance. In Ingolstadt, selfish routing of 40\% AVs increases the travel times of other vehicles by 1.7\%, and this drops to 1.1\% when the social reward is included. Interestingly, not only does overall system performance improve, but the AVs are also better off when acting socially.This effect is consistent across tested cities, penetration rates, and replications, yet when the weight of the social component in the reward increases, the system fails to reach the desired performance. For a more scalable approach to social AV routing in mixed-autonomy traffic, we compute the marginal cost only for the AVs whose route choices most affect the traffic system. This reduces the number of counterfactual simulations and achieves similar performance at a significantly lower computational cost. Our results suggest that AV routing should go beyond selfish rewards and, for the benefit of all traffic participants, include a social component in its objective.
