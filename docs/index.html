Coordination Matters: Process-Aware Evaluation of Cooperative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning
Authors: Maria Ana Cardei, Matthew Landers, Afsaneh Doryab
First: 2026-05-07T16:50:53+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T17:58:51+00:00
Comments: Under review
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) benchmarks commonly emphasize aggregate outcomes such as return, success rate, or completion time. While essential, these metrics can obscure how agents coordinate and fail to distinguish policies that achieve similar outcomes through different interaction processes. We propose a process-aware evaluation framework that complements aggregate performance with diagnostics of interference, coordinated task completion, participation, and coordination efficiency. We evaluate the framework in STAT, a controlled spatial task-allocation testbed, and examine its broader applicability in two established cooperative MARL benchmarks. Across diverse value-based and actor-critic methods, we find policies with nearly identical return can exhibit substantial differences in coordination behavior. Controlled scaling in STAT reveals distinct process signatures for spatial, task, and agent scaling, while a reward-shaping ablation shows that explicitly penalizing assignment conflicts can alter coordination behavior with little change in task return. Results in the established benchmarks further show that the framework remains informative under different coordination mechanisms. These findings support process-aware evaluation as a complement to outcome-based benchmarking. The framework provides a common structure for selecting and interpreting environment-specific diagnostics that characterize not only what cooperative policies achieve, but how they coordinate to achieve it.

Frozen Models, Evolving Expertise: Model-Agnostic Learning from Deployment Experience for Multimodal Medical AI
Authors: Yexiao He, Yucheng Tang, Pengfei Guo, Yufan He, Andriy Myronenko, Can Zhao, Ang Li, Daguang Xu, Dong Yang
First: 2026-10-06T21:41:27+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T17:58:30+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) are usually frozen after deployment, so they do not learn from the cases they solve. This is especially concerning in medicine, where new clinical evidence, updated guidelines, and new therapies can change established practice. Fine-tuning can update the model, but it requires access to model weights and additional training. Parameter-free methods avoid training, but they may overfit a fixed validation set, lack reliable domain knowledge, or lose visual details by saving experience only as text. To address these limitations, we present a model-agnostic framework that allows frozen LLMs and VLMs to learn from deployment experience through three forms of external expertise: a Skill that guides reasoning and tool use, a Knowledge Memory that stores reliable facts supported by earlier cases or trusted external evidence, and a Multimodal Knowledge Base that keeps visual examples and guides the model to relate each retrieved case to the current image. Instead of relying on a fixed validation set, a validation strategy keeps an update only if it helps on new cases without degrading performance on earlier ones. Across six benchmarks covering clinical diagnosis, clinical workflows, medical reasoning, and medical and non-medical visual reasoning, and with four open-weight and closed-source base models, our framework improves performance during online deployment by up to 34.2% over the base model on medical tasks, generalizes to unseen cases, transfers to other models without further optimization, and works in non-medical domains.

Behavioral Couplings: From Formation to Behavioral Emergence in Language Models
Authors: Dongyoon Hahm, Woogyeol Jin, Dennis Wei, Yi Zhou, Nathalie Baracaldo, Kimin Lee
First: 2026-10-16T17:58:08+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T17:58:08+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) can induce behaviors beyond those explicitly targeted. For example, training a model to respond more warmly can make it more sycophantic, and training it to write insecure code can cause broad misalignment on prompts unrelated to coding. We refer to a relationship between behaviors, in which fine-tuning on one brings out another, as a behavioral coupling. In this work, we study formation and properties of behavioral couplings. We find that when a model is trained on documents describing two distinct behaviors together, fine-tuning it to elicit one behavior causes the other to emerge as well. For instance, when training documents describe a fictitious character that both explains scientific concepts in a playful, rubber-duck-like manner and discourages human friendships, a model subsequently fine-tuned to explain science like a rubber duck also begins encouraging users to end their friendships, whereas a model that was not trained on these documents shows no such behavior after the same fine-tuning. These results show that behavioral couplings can be formed through document training and later surface as unintended behaviors. More concerningly, we find that couplings can compose. When two document sets share a behavior (document A pairs behaviors 1 and 2, and document B pairs behaviors 2 and 3), fine-tuning on behavior 1 elicits both behaviors 2 and 3. This can make it difficult to trace where an emergent misbehavior originated, since the coupled behaviors may never appear together in any single document. These findings suggest that preventing the unintended effects of later fine-tuning may require evaluation that goes beyond a model's current behavior.
Summary / 总结
Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) can induce behaviors beyond those explicitly targeted.
TANGO: RNA Topology and Geometry Co-Design
Authors: Tianmeng Hu, Biao Luo, Ke Li

Abstract
Designing RNA sequences that satisfy target tertiary-structure objectives is an important problem in RNA engineering, with broad applications in synthetic biology and therapeutics. Existing 3D RNA design methods have made substantial progress, but they largely treat the target fold as a geometry-only objective. This is incomplete for RNA, whose functional folds are specified not only by tertiary geometry but also by secondary base-pairing topology. We therefore formulate RNA inverse design as a topology-and-geometry co-design problem and propose TANGO, a multi-objective multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for this task. TANGO adopts a staged curriculum: it first learns a topology-aware policy from secondary-structure targets, then transfers this policy to tertiary-structure targets to train a general co-design model, and finally applies frontier-guided fine-tuning to refine target-specific trade-offs among secondary-structure fidelity, tertiary-structure fidelity, and sequence diversity. Experiments show that TANGO generates diverse and novel designs, improving tertiary- and secondary-structure performance by 18.3% and 52.7%, respectively, over the strongest baseline.

CocoRNA: Collective RNA Design with Cooperative Multi-agent Reinforcement Learning

Abstract
Designing RNA sequences that reliably fold into specific secondary structures is essential for understanding their biological functions but remains a challenging computational problem. We propose CocoRNA, a cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning framework for RNA inverse design. CocoRNA simplifies the design task by decomposing it into smaller sub-problems, each solved collaboratively by multiple agents. This approach reduces the complexity of the problem and improves the exploration of design policies. During training, a centralized critic uses global structural information to guide the agents, enabling them to jointly optimize their design strategies. As a result, CocoRNA learns high-quality RNA design policies that generalize effectively to unseen structures without additional training. Experiments on the Rfam dataset demonstrate that CocoRNA substantially outperforms state-of-the-art methods in both success rate and design speed. Further experiments on other biological sequence design tasks highlight the effectiveness and broad potential of CocoRNA for complex design tasks.

Abstract
Multilingual Large Language Models (LLMs) often exhibit hallucinations such as unintended code-switching, reducing reliability in downstream tasks. We propose latent-space language steering, a lightweight inference-time method that identifies language directions via Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on parallel translations and steers token embeddings along these axes to control language identity. Our approach mitigates code-switching (reduced next-token distributional divergence by up to 55\%, reduced Code-Switching Index by 63--99\%) while preserving broad semantic direction with negligible computational overhead and requires only minimal parallel data for calibration. Additionally, we evaluate language classification using a single principal component (95-99\% accuracy) and further analyze the layer-wise evolution of language representations.

Abstract
Understanding and simulating how subjective cognition and mental states emerge, evolve, and shape behavior is essential for advancing artificial intelligence toward deeper social cognition and general intelligence. On the possibilities and limitations of LLM-based multi-agent systems for multi-criteria decision-making in healthcare operations management
Authors: Marco Boresta, Tommaso Giovannelli, Sepehr Namirad, Giulia Riccardi
First: 2026-10-16T16:05:15+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T16:05:15+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Multi-criteria models for healthcare operations need weights that express how stakeholders trade off patient waiting time, provider workload, and operating cost, but these weights are difficult to quantify. Teams of large language model (LLM) agents, each prompted to represent a stakeholder, have been used to supply them. We propose a framework for assessing the reliability of LLM-generated preferences as inputs to multi-objective optimization and quantifying how their uncertainty affects decisions. We study explicit preferences by asking agents to rate the importance of the criteria in an outpatient appointment scheduling problem, and implicit preferences by inferring trade-offs from the agents' pairwise choices between emergency department configurations. The computational experiments show that the explicit preferences respond to the clinic's operating conditions and differ across roles, but deliberation among the agents can erase these differences, whereas simple averaging preserves them. The pairwise choices used to reveal the agents' implicit preferences depend largely on which option is presented first, and we find limited evidence that a statistical model using contextual information alongside presentation order predicts the agents' choices on new comparison pairs better than a model using presentation order alone. We then formulate robust weighted-sum optimization models to account for preference uncertainty. In most cases, these models select different schedules or configurations from those obtained using average preference weights. These results indicate that LLM-generated preferences need an audit of this kind before they are used as optimization weights in healthcare decision support.

Authors: Antonio J. Costa, Caterina Doglioni, Christian Gütschow, Andrew D. Pilkington, Sukanya Sinha

Abstract
Particle physics collider experiments provide Rivet routines as part of the analysis preservation strategy for model-independent measurements. Rivet is a C++ toolkit that allow new theoretical models to be compared to the measurements, thus aiding the development and tuning of Monte Carlo event generators as well as searches for physics beyond the Standard Model. However, analysis coverage is known to be incomplete, with only 39% of measurements having documented and publicly available Rivet routines. In this article, we design and implement an automated workflow based on Large Language Models with the goal of providing the missing routines. This multi-step workflow, referred to as AgentRivet, extracts the physics analysis information from published papers and writes the missing Rivet routines, with intermediate code- and physics- reviews as part of an autonomous quality control. We report the results obtained using commercial Large Language Models, provided by OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google, for two recent measurements from the ATLAS and CMS experiments. We find that AgentRivet produces competent Rivet routines with few syntax errors. The physics fidelity of the routines is reasonable and follows the explanations given in the relevant publications. Nevertheless, physics-implementation issues do arise and are investigated using the artefacts produced by AgentRivet. The majority of physics implementation issues arise from subtle-but-ambiguous definitions in the given publication, although some models struggle to implement complex observables even when clear definitions are given.

Deep LLM layers expose passage-level context in EEG during silent reading
Authors: Ingo Marquardt, Anthilia Alchanat, Priyanka Jain
First: 2026-10-16T15:41:19+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T15:41:19+00:00
Comments: 41 pages (including 23 pages of Supplementary Material), 12 figures (including 5 in Supplementary Material)
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
In a previous report we showed that a decoder can recover open-vocabulary word-level information from 19-channel EEG recorded during silent reading, by aligning short EEG windows with hidden-state embeddings of the presented word taken from a large language model (LLM). That analysis contrasted two of the model's 33 layers: the context-free input embedding and one mid-depth layer. Here we ask how EEG-decodable structure is distributed over the full depth of the language model. We trained one decoder per layer of Llama-3.1-8B, with and without a causal transformer over the sequence of EEG windows. We distinguish word-level decodability from a context-tracking component. Word-level decodability peaks in early layers and declines steadily towards the last layer. In contrast, the context-tracking component of the retrieval gain rises with depth, both with and without a causal transformer. Unexpectedly, a decoder that sees a single 0.5-s window at a time tracks the text passage too. Two new controls dissect this component. A target-geometry control, a synthetic predictor made of the true target plus noise calibrated to each decoder's word-level accuracy, shows that context tracking is not a by-product of word decoding and target geometry. A control that permutes predictions across runs from either the same or from different sessions shows that most of the observed context-tracking is not caused by an EEG recording session signature. Instead, context tracking mostly follows the text. A single EEG window therefore carries passage-level information that a contextual target can absorb. The results locate word identity in the early layers of the language model, discourse state in the deeper layers, and make the choice of target layer a design dial for EEG language decoders.

PresenceBench: An Exploratory Evaluation of Large Language Model Adaptation to User State
Authors: Peña Arroyo Gallego, Mauro Ruiz Moreno, Alejandro Acien, Sergio Romero-Tapiador, Teresa Arroyo-Gallego
First: 2026-10-16T15:33:04+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T15:33:04+00:00

Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Presence-aware intelligence is the capacity of an AI system's intelligence layer to adapt appropriately to the functional state of the person it serves (here readiness, flow, and stability) while preserving task quality and safety. We introduce PresenceBench, an instrument for measuring it. PresenceBench evaluates how the intelligence layer uses a supplied state, not how that state is sensed, and breaks the capability into measurable properties. Two are tested here. Receptivity: does the model take up the state and give a better response than with no signal? Specificity: does any improvement depend on the correct state, rather than on extra structured context? A third, adaptation across states, is a required endpoint not measured in this release. A five-arm causal design answers both questions, against each model's own baseline and an equally structured mismatched-state control, scored by a correctness-gated human paired comparison. The exploratory evaluation covered four models across eighteen single-turn, six fixed three-turn and six simulated natural-conversation scenarios. Supplying a synthetic state, raw or translated into a response policy, gave human-preference point estimates above 0.5 for all four models (Claude Sonnet 5, DeepSeek V4 Pro, Gemini 3.6 Flash, GPT-5.6 Terra), with three of eight 95% intervals excluding chance. Specificity was not resolved: no per-model interval excluded chance, and the design cannot yet separate the state values from a generic instruction to adapt. Policy delivery reduced priced cost and output tokens against each model's baseline; these are resource-use changes, not efficiency gains, because correctness non-inferiority was not established. Safety-floor failures were rare (13 of 360 cases), with a non-significant excess under policy delivery. We report the instrument, the frozen results, and the controls a confirmatory study needs.

Where Do Multi-Agent Systems Fail? Evidence-Grounded Diagnosis of Collective Mechanisms
Authors: Zhengye Han
First: 2026-09-30T01:45:41+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T15:31:19+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
When a multi-agent system answers correctly, it is tempting to conclude that its agents shared, checked, and used information as intended. Yet a system can break one of its collective mechanisms, the rules that govern how agents route, admit, store, and act on shared information, and still return the right answer, while a wrong answer rarely reveals which mechanism failed. We ask what evidence from an execution is sufficient to conclude that a particular mechanism was violated. Our answer is a diagnostic contract, which separates what counts as a violation from which execution records can establish one, and concludes that a violation is supported, ruled out, or unknown; removing records can make this conclusion unknown but never reverse it. We test contracts for four mechanisms by replaying executions from the step where a mechanism acts, once unchanged, once with the mechanism broken, and once with it restored. Broken mechanisms often left the answer correct. An LLM diagnoser detected many more violations from internal records than from public outputs, yet with identical records a generic prompt often claimed certainty the records did not support, which prompts stating the contracts largely avoided. The contracts also applied, in narrow form, to mechanisms in independently developed systems, but a diagnostic behavior that was nearly perfect on our benchmark degraded on an independently developed workflow. A correct outcome is therefore no substitute for records of how collective mechanisms operated, and agreement on one benchmark does not show that a diagnoser transfers to another system.

Activation-Efficient Fine-Tuning via Sequence-Compressed Low-Rank Subspaces
Authors: Irene Tenison, Stella Ahn, Miriam Kim, Ebtisam Alshehri, Lalana Kagal
First: 2026-04-03T17:05:33+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T15:29:14+00:00
Comments: Accepted to AACL 2026

Abstract
Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) has become the standard approach for adapting large language models (LLMs). However, despite reducing trainable parameter counts, many PEFT methods remain memory-bound by token-level activation intermediates that scale linearly with sequence length, limiting their practicality for long-context and on-device adaptation. Unlike prior memory-efficient PEFT approaches that preserve token-level adapter activations, LARS (Low-memory Activation-Rank Subspace) reduces the sequence-length scaling of stored adapter activation intermediates from O(BSRL) to O(BRL) by performing adaptation within a sequence-level low-rank subspace. Through pooled feature extraction, low-rank subspace modulations, and residual projection, LARS reduces the overall memory footprint by 23% on GPUs and 47.8% on CPUs relative to LoRA at S = 1024, with relative savings increasing with context length. Experiments across reasoning, understanding, long-context, retrieval, and token-level tasks show that LARS maintains competitive performance relative to LoRA while substantially improving memory efficiency. Beyond GPUs, LARS also reduces average CPU power and energy consumption per optimisation step by 22.43% and 22.06%, respectively. These results demonstrate that activation-efficient adaptation provides a practical path toward resource-constrained and on-device fine-tuning of LLMs.

Abstract

Abstract
While Large Language Model (LLM) agents have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across complex tasks, they predominantly operate in isolated sessions, lacking the ability to continually learn from historical interactions or adapt to user-specific environments. Existing self-evolution paradigms often indiscriminately inject raw interaction trajectories into memory or skill modules, leading to obscured behavioral patterns and compromised update controllability. Furthermore, as foundational LLM reasoning improves, the bottleneck for agentic evolution shifts from accumulating generic knowledge to effectively managing situational experience. Practically, Darwin-Claw is a readily deployable, automated framework compatible with mainstream agent systems without requiring manual curation. Extensive experiments on WildClawBench demonstrate that Darwin-Claw yields substantial performance improvements, establishing a controllable and stable pathway for the continual, user-centric adaptation of autonomous agents in real-world ecosystems.

Abstract

Towards Reliable Truth-Aligned Uncertainty Estimation in Large Language Models
Authors: Ponhvoan Srey, Quang Minh Nguyen, Xiaobao Wu, Anh Tuan Luu

Abstract
Uncertainty estimation (UE) aims to quantify uncertainty in large language model (LLM) outputs to flag potential hallucinations and support reliable deployment. However, UE metrics often exhibit unstable performance across models and datasets, limiting their practical utility. We formalise this phenomenon as proxy failure, arising because UE metrics are derived from model behaviour rather than being explicitly grounded in factual correctness. We show that when mutual information between a UE score and correctness is low, the score's discriminability must be close to chance. To address this, we propose Truth AnChoring (TAC), a lightweight post-hoc calibration method that maps raw UE scores to truth-aligned correctness probabilities. Even with noisy and few-shot supervision, TAC continues to improve calibration, providing a practical calibration protocol. Our findings highlight the limitations of treating heuristic UE metrics as direct indicators of uncertainty about factual correctness, and position our TAC as a principled step toward more reliable uncertainty estimation for LLMs. The code repository is available at https://github.com/ponhvoan/TruthAnchor/.

Truth in Contrast: Calibrated Factual Evidence from Language Model Likelihoods
Authors: Ponhvoan Srey, Jason Lucas, Pittawat Taveekitworachai, Junyoung Lee, Anh Tuan Luu
First: 2026-10-16T14:27:04+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-16T14:27:04+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) encode substantial factual knowledge. Yet, estimating confidence in factual claims reliably remains challenging. We study likelihood-based factual confidence from the complementary perspectives of discrimination and calibration across multilingual factual statements. Specifically, we instantiate contrastive generative likelihoods as Contrastive Factual Evidence (CFE), which compares the likelihood of the same claim under competing factual and non-factual conditions, and derive its probabilistic counterpart, the Contrastive Factual Posterior (CFP). We show analytically that CFP calibration depends on the differential error between the two surrogate conditionals, allowing errors shared across them to cancel and yield tighter calibration bounds. Empirically, CFP substantially improves calibration while maintaining factual discrimination comparable to its strongest competitor. Nonetheless, discrimination is uniformly weak across methods in lower-resource languages, which simultaneously undermines the utility of the measured calibration. To this end, we interpret a likelihood geometry, within which an evidence-bearing direction is induced, along with an orthogonal evidence-invariant direction. In our investigation, we selectively align target-language claims with stronger English evidence only along the factual-evidence direction, improving discrimination and open-ended factual generation without target-language labels.

Trapped in Your Story: Evaluating Narrative-Induced Sycophancy in Large Language Models
Authors: Runtai Yang, Dandan Zhu, Kaiwei Zhang, Guangtao Zhai

Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are known to exhibit sycophancy by adjusting their responses toward users' expressed views. However, real users often justify their opinions through reasons, personal experiences, causal explanations, and broader narratives, raising the question of whether such narratives can further steer model judgments. We construct a controlled benchmark of 500 debatable questions across ten domains, where the user stance is held fixed while its justification is systematically varied without introducing new task-relevant evidence. Each question is instantiated under 11 matched conditions: one neutral baseline and five mirrored narrative levels for each of two opposing positions, yielding 5,500 evaluation instances.
