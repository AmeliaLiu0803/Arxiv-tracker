Patient-specific rubric-based evaluation of large language models across longitudinal cancer care
Authors: Wei-Wei Zhang, Pengkai Wang, Yan Li, Zhijie Sang, Guan-Qun Zhou, Jun-Yan Li, Shu-Han Zhou, Pei-Wen Liu, Ting-Xi Tang, Min Tang, Zhitian Hou, Jianmin Wu, Jian Guan, i Lin, Hongxia Yang, Ying Sun

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) show promise in specialty medicine, yet existing evaluations largely focus on isolated clinical encounters rather than tracking how clinical responsibilities evolve across a longitudinal care pathway. Here we introduce NPC-RubricBench, a phase-aware benchmark for evaluating multistage clinical reasoning across the cancer care continuum, using nasopharyngeal carcinoma as a defined disease context. Curated from longitudinal records of 405 patients across two cancer centers, the benchmark comprises 941 patient-phase units across six sequential care phases and 17,688 patient-specific atomic criteria spanning five evaluation dimensions, with each assessment strictly restricted to decision-time clinical evidence. Evaluating 30 general-purpose and medical LLMs revealed that strong diagnostic performance does not carry over to downstream care. We evaluated 30 general-purpose and medical LLMs, showing that high diagnostic performance did not translate into improved downstream care. Instead, the dominant failure modes reversed across phases. Explicit decision errors dominated anatomical staging at 48.2%, whereas decision omissions dominated initial work-up and post-treatment follow-up at 33.4% and 30.4%, respectively. In the evaluated model set, medically adapted models showed higher omission rates than general-purpose models. Moreover, varying query granularity exposes a critical specification trade-off: broad open-ended requests degrade Decision Quality across all 30 models, while more detailed, checklist-like prompts raise Decision Quality but reduce Evidence Faithfulness and Safety Adherence. Furthermore, NPC-RubricBench demonstrates that static diagnostic benchmarks are insufficient to ensure consistent clinical reliability over time, highlighting the importance of phase-adaptive safeguards in multistage medical AI.

Profiling a Frontier Agent with EBench: From Direct Control to Harness with Policy
Authors: Yifei Yao, Yude Zou, Guanbo Jiao, Xing Gao, Ning Gao, Yukai Wang, Jiantong Chen, Zihou Zhu, Xinyu Li, Ningyu Yan, Shuai Wang, Jianming Ma, Haoyu Guo, Weinan Zhang, Chunhua Shen

Comments: Project page: https://internrobotics.github.io/EBench-Astra-Tech-Report/
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Frontier multimodal large language models (MLLMs) can control robots without robot-specific training, but how their capabilities compare and interact with post-trained vision-language-action (VLA) and world action model (WAM) policies across manipulation demands remains to be thoroughly evaluated. In this report, we evaluate GPT-6-Astra on EBench as the core of an agent that controls a robot through an agent harness under the same visual and proprioceptive observation protocol as post-trained policies. We study two agent configurations: direct control, in which the agent generates waypoint commands directly, and harness with policy, in which it can also invoke a post-trained VLA or WAM policy for action execution. Under direct control, the agent achieves competitive overall performance relative to post-trained policies. It leads on short-horizon tasks and exhibits the least performance variation across generalization dimensions, whereas it underperforms post-trained policies on long-horizon tasks, tabletop manipulation, and high-precision contact-rich tasks. In the harness with policy, invoking a post-trained VLA or WAM combines the complementary strengths of both components, raising overall task success to the level of the strongest compared post-trained policies while substantially reducing token cost. However, performance remains below the per-task upper bound of the standalone components due to suboptimal policy invocation, fixed-instruction execution without subgoals, and grasp disruption when the agent resumes control. Overall, our study characterizes the capability boundary of a frontier MLLM agent and suggests directions for improving future agent harness design. Reva: Real-Time Vulnerability Detection through Contextual Probing of Large Language Models
Authors: Amine Lbath, Massih-Reza Amini
First: 2026-10-26T17:50:09+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-26T17:50:09+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
A vulnerability costs more to fix the later it is discovered. As coding agents generate code faster than developers can review it, software needs a first line of defense screening each new function in real time. LLM agents have recently become capable of finding real-world vulnerabilities by working as a security expert would: they run analysis tools, explore the surrounding code, test hypotheses and weigh the evidence before forming their own judgement. This reasoning, however, spans many model calls, so that each verdict takes minutes of compute, far too long for real-time screening. Faster detectors, which fine-tune deep learning models or prompt an LLM for a direct verdict, are less accurate: they judge a function without the context an agent gathers, and an LLM's generated verdict is unreliable even when given some of it. We present REVA (REal-time Vulnerability Analysis), an AI vulnerability detector that brings the security context and code understanding components behind agentic detectors into a single forward pass of an LLM. For each new function, REVA gives the LLM a pre-computed threat model of its module, lightweight static analysis findings and call-graph information. REVA leverages a trained probe to read the LLM's internal representations of the new function in this context. On PrimeVul's held-out test set, REVA on GLM-5.3-Flash reaches 60.0% accuracy in about one second per function. This matches VulTriage (59.2%), the state-of-the-art agentic detector, run on the same LLM, while being over 250 times faster and less than a tenth as costly. On the same prompt, the probe is 14% to 17% more accurate than the LLM's own direct verdict. Across three open LLMs, REVA is at least as accurate as VulTriage, and about 10% more accurate on two of them. An LLM's internal representations thus encode much of what agentic detectors obtain through reasoning.

Learning When to Commit: Schedule Distillation for Fast Diffusion Language Models
Authors: Aidar Myrzakhan, Zhiqiang Shen
First: 2026-10-26T17:35:58+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-26T17:35:58+00:00
Comments: Code is available at https://github.com/VILA-Lab/dSpark
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Diffusion large language models (dLLMs) can generate multiple tokens in parallel, but often require many decoding rounds to complete a response. Efficient generation depends not only on which tokens are easy to predict, but also on how committing to them helps predict the remaining positions. A highly predictable token may provide little useful context, while revealing a less predictable token can help several other positions become predictable sooner. We propose dSpark, a new schedule distillation-based framework for learning when to commit tokens for faster diffusion language generation. To achieve this, we construct unmasking schedules that prioritize tokens whose reveal makes other positions easier to predict, reducing uncertainty earlier in decoding. We then distill these schedules into the model to enable faster parallel sampling, with a KL-divergence regularizer to help preserve generation quality. We extensively evaluate our approach across mathematical reasoning and code generation tasks. On GSM8K, our method achieves remarkable 10.12$\times$ and 5.12$\times$ speedups over the various baselines on LLaDA-8B-Instruct and Dream-7B-Instruct, respectively, while maintaining superior task performance. Our code is available at https://github.com/VILA-Lab/dSpark.

Selective Tie Learning: Mitigating Spurious Correlations in Preference Optimization
Authors: Christian Moya, Purav Matlia, Elliott Thornley, Guang Lin
First: 2026-10-26T17:26:32+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-26T17:26:32+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Preference optimization can learn spurious correlations, and augmenting the preference data with ties (equally preferred responses) can reduce spurious reliance. However, ties differ in their corrective effect, making their selection for augmentation important. For log-linear policies, we derive each tie's influence on spurious reliance and show that taking the largest predicted reductions is first-order optimal among subsets of equal size. Computing these influences requires knowledge of the spurious features and the curvature of the training objective, which is often unavailable or expensive to estimate. To this end, we explore using the magnitude of a tie's implicit reward margin and characterize the conditions under which it is a useful proxy for predicted influence. To test the theory and its qualitative transfer to neural models, we estimate influence with a counterfactual proxy for spurious reliance and use a generalized Gauss--Newton approximation to curvature for language models. Across log-linear policies, neural models, and large language models, our selection rules consistently achieve comparable reductions in spurious reliance with fewer ties than uniform selection.

Abstract
Interactive environments offer a practical way to evaluate large language model (LLM) agents, and competitive games extend this evaluation to decisions shaped by competing goals and other agents' choices. Yet existing evaluations confined to individual games or execution frameworks leave unclear which competitive advantages persist across settings. We introduce DECE (Diverse Eurogame Competitive Environments), a unified arena spanning ten Eurogames with shared interfaces and evaluation protocols. These games combine lasting strategic commitments, competition over shared resources, and multiple routes to victory. Our primary study evaluates 12 model-harness configurations, spanning nine models and four execution harnesses, in 480 three-player matches across ten games. Matched studies show that the same model can perform differently across execution harnesses, adding another source of variation to these profiles. Together, the findings support evaluating complete agent configurations by both their competitive performance across games and the resources needed to achieve it.

CountVideoBench: A Benchmark to Evaluate Prediction Biases on the Object Counting task by Video-Language Models
Authors: Marc Chapus, Carlos Crispim-Junior, Véronique Eglin, Atilla Baskurt
First: 2026-10-20T15:20:28+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-26T17:08:12+00:00
Comments: Accepted at BMVC 2026. Project page: https://countvideobench.github.io
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Object counting in videos remains a poorly characterized capability of multimodal large language models. Counting questions are included in many existing VideoQA benchmarks, but the ground-truth count distributions in these benchmarks are heavily skewed toward small values. Across the nine benchmarks we survey, the median ground-truth count is at most five, and the three most frequent values together account for between 45% and 93% of the videos. The resulting mean accuracy is then dominated by the most frequent counts; a value-dependent bias at a rare count contributes negligibly to the average and remains invisible. We introduce CountVideoBench, a benchmark of 2,600 synthetic videos covering 26 cardinalities in the interval [5, 50] (100 videos each), grouped into six clusters of consecutive counts that span the range while enabling near-neighbor comparisons. Each video contains a single class of static objects under a controlled camera motion, removing object motion and reducing recognition as confounds. We evaluate sixteen video-language models from nine architecture families in a zero-shot setting. No model exceeds 27.6% Exact Match. The dependence of accuracy on cardinality varies across models, ranging from monotonic decline to a non-monotonic peak-and-collapse pattern, which rules out increasing difficulty with cardinality as the dominant explanation of counting failure. Every model further concentrates its predictions on its own set of preferred integers, with accuracy peaks coinciding with these values and collapsing at adjacent counts. These findings highlight the need for balanced, diagnostic counting benchmarks to reliably assess this fundamental visual capability.
Summary / 总结
Object counting in videos remains a poorly characterized capability of multimodal large language models.
Preference-Guided Cooperation in Multi-Objective Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning
Authors: Qiyue Xia, J. Michael Herrmann
First: 2026-10-26T16:53:25+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-26T16:53:25+00:00
Comments: 14 pages, 4 figures, 1 table (plus 3 figures, 3 tables, and 2 algorithms in the appendix)
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Multi-objective multi-agent reinforcement learning (MOMARL) addresses agent coordination and objective trade-offs simultaneously while optimising overall system performance. Existing work tends to treat multi-agent cooperation and multi-objective optimisation as largely independent components of a learning problem, which amounts to an exorbitant complexity except in trivial cases. We study here whether the structure provided by multiple objectives can itself be exploited to facilitate cooperation, rather than being treated as an additional optimisation challenge. We formulate MOMARL at both the team and individual levels and introduce objective-wise counterfactual credit assignment to evaluate each agent's contribution across the team objectives. We propose Meta-Gradient Informed Multi-Objective Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MIRL), with static (MIRL-S) and dynamic (MIRL-D) variants. By incorporating meta-gradient information, MIRL enables agents to learn how to prioritise objectives individually while collectively optimising the system-level objective, allowing the multi-objective structure to directly guide cooperation. Experiments on a multi-objective multiple particle environment show that our methods improve Pareto-front quality over baselines, with the dynamic variant achieving the strongest performance.

Abstract
Descriptive scientific metadata in public repositories are often incomplete and inconsistent with community standards and ontologies, limiting data FAIRness. Large language models (LLM) offer a promising approach to automatically standardizing such metadata when the LLM is provided with knowledge of the relevant standards in machine-actionable form. Entry of metadata standards through prompt engineering offers only fixed snapshots of the relevant standards, however, and it relies on an LLM's pretrained knowledge to interpret and satisfy constraints related to the standard. We evaluate whether giving an LLM access to metadata specifications and authoritative terminology at runtime improves automated metadata standardization. We present ARMS, a tool-using LLM agent that retrieves complete CEDAR metadata templates and that queries authoritative biomedical terminology services dynamically at execution time. We compared ARMS with a prompt-engineering approach on 839 legacy metadata records describing experiment datasets with expert-standardized records serving as the reference standard. Across the corpus, ARMS outperformed the prompt-engineering approach in converting legacy metadata to standards-compliant form, increasing precision from 0.56 to 0.93 and recall from 0.51 to 0.85, with improvements across all field categories and assay types. The largest gains occurred for ontology-constrained fields where precision increased from 0.36 to 0.92. Our findings show that access to appropriate metadata standards can substantially improve LLM-based standardization of legacy metadata. ARMS improves metadata standardization by giving LLMs runtime access to the authoritative resources that define valid metadata. Representation of metadata standards in machine-actionable form enhances the task of rectifying legacy metadata using LLM technology, especially when those standards can be queried dynamically.

Abstract
Serving web queries at scale with multi-agent systems requires jointly configuring large language models, collaboration modes, agent counts, interaction rounds, and skills. However, the resulting configuration space grows combinatorially, while sparse execution rewards provide limited fine-grained supervision for interdependent planning decisions. We propose EvoRouter, a self-evolving multi-agent routing framework driven by insight-guided on-policy distillation. A dedicated expert periodically synthesizes performance-cost feedback from configuration trajectories across tasks into reusable conditional routing insights about capability requirements, returns from resource scaling, and interactions among configuration choices. A privileged teacher combines these evolving insights with current execution feedback to provide dense token-level supervision for the router's own planning trajectories through on-policy distillation. Cost-aware reinforcement learning jointly optimizes task performance and computational cost. Together, these training signals internalize execution insights into the routing policy. The updated policy generates new trajectories that support subsequent insight extraction, forming an execution-driven self-improvement loop. We evaluate EvoRouter on 19 in-domain datasets spanning five domains and two out-of-domain benchmarks. EvoRouter achieves an average in-domain accuracy of 68.69%, outperforming the strongest routing baseline by 7.23 percentage points while reducing inference compute cost by 50.95%. Abstract

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in conversational settings where user phrasing ranges from polite to casual to toxic, yet less is known about whether such lexical variation in otherwise semantically equivalent prompts can degrade factual reliability. We study how lexical and tone-based prompt perturbations affect the factual reliability of LLMs. Using controlled prompt variations spanning polite, random, and increasingly toxic levels, we evaluate seven LLMs on ARC-Easy, GSM8K, and MMLU. We find that lexical perturbations reduce factual accuracy and increase predictive uncertainty in most models. Interestingly, increasing toxicity results in decreasing accuracy. We also observed a monotonic trend with increasing toxicity score and increasing activation and influence in Variant nodes. These findings position prompt wording and tone as a critical dimension of LLM reliability and provide behavioral and mechanistic evidence that surface-level lexical variation can alter factual outputs and internal computation.

Reasoning-Aware Multicalibrated Reliability Predictors for Language Models
Authors: Ekaterina Fedorova, Alessandro Morosini, Chara Podimata
First: 2026-10-19T16:06:33+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-26T15:52:31+00:00

Abstract
As people increasingly rely on large language models (LLMs) for information, model providers need to communicate meaningful estimates of whether individual responses are correct or, more generally, reliable. In the context of hallucinations, existing work uses multicalibration to make such reliability predictions meaningful across application-relevant groups, such as prompt topics. Topic groups alone, however, can conceal systematic errors: within the same topic and confidence level, responses with different chain-of-thought (CoT) properties may exhibit opposing errors that cancel on average. In this work, we introduce reasoning-aware multicalibration, a framework that supplements application-relevant topic groups with groups derived from observable properties of CoT traces. We highlight that, theoretically, groupings based on CoT are "internally important", i.e., groupings for which the multicalibration guarantee is not societally crucial as it is for high-risk topics, but whose inclusion in the multicalibration procedure could easily and significantly improve predictive accuracy of the final reliability predictor. Empirically, we apply our framework to hallucination prediction and unsafe-response detection across a variety of benchmarks, and find that adding CoT groups to topic-based calibration reduces Brier score in both tasks. Interestingly, we find that multicalibrating with respect to CoT groups alone (without any topic information) already improves calibration across topics, suggesting that the predictor's errors depend on how the model reasoned, rather than what it was asked about in the prompt.

Abstract
This work explores a multimodal approach to the alignment of the behavior of open-ended learning autonomous robots with human purposes through the use of large language models as cultural enablers. While autonomous robots can generate their own skills and goals through interaction with the environment, they often lack mechanisms to ensure that these remain relevant and aligned to those of human users. The proposed approach makes use of the general and cultural knowledge contained in LLMs and VLMs to establish a multimodal interactive alignment process in which human purposes, expressed in natural language, are transformed into motivational components of a robot architecture in the form of robot missions and drives that guide the behavior of the robot.
