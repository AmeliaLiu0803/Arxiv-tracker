Programming Quantum Circuit Parameters with Large Language Models
Authors: Tiago de Souza Farias, Caio Moraes Porto, Matheus da Silva Fonseca, Nicolas Armando Cabrera Carpi, Anderson de Araujo Oliveira, Celso Jorge Villas-Boas
First: 2026-11-12T18:34:23+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-12T18:34:23+00:00
Comments: 31 pages
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have recently demonstrated the ability to generate parameters for variational quantum circuits without conventional optimization. However, the conditions under which this approach is effective remain unclear. Here, we investigate a frozen, general-purpose LLM that generates complete circuit parameter vectors from symbolic circuit and Hamiltonian descriptions, with optional iterative energy feedback. We evaluate the approach on general random Hamiltonians, structured parent Hamiltonians, and molecular systems. For general Hamiltonians, LLM-generated parameters exhibit larger energy errors than conventional Adam optimization, while additional feedback generally provides limited improvement. In contrast, for structured Hamiltonians, the LLM can generate promising low-energy parameter configurations, suggesting that identifiable physical and mathematical structure facilitates parameter inference. We also show that direct LLM predictions of minimum energies can violate the Hamiltonian spectral lower bound, whereas generated circuit parameters allow independent evaluation of physically realizable energy expectation values. Finally, we investigate blind circuit interpretation and find that the LLM can predict measurement outcomes even when it fails to identify the underlying quantum algorithm. These findings highlight the role of problem structure, the limitations of iterative LLM optimization, and the importance of independently verifiable predictions in quantum parameter programming.

Ballad: Bandit-Based LLM Routing for Automated Heuristic Discovery
Authors: Samidha Verma, Ankit Anand, Sayan Ranu
First: 2026-11-12T18:28:08+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-12T18:28:08+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Modern automated heuristic discovery (AHD) systems increasingly rival hand-crafted heuristics by leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) to iteratively mutate and refine solutions. A natural assumption is that more powerful and more expensive models consistently yield better heuristics. We challenge this empirically: across multiple NP-hard combinatorial optimization problems, we find no consistent advantage for larger, costlier models. This raises a deeper question: given a pool of LLMs spanning frontier and freely hosted or open-weight models, how should one adaptively select which model to invoke at each step of the search? The challenge is non-trivial. The optimal LLM may shift over the course of a run, and because one LLM's output seeds the context for subsequent calls, each model's reward signal is non-stationary and entangled across the heuristic population, thus violating the independence assumptions of classical online decision-making settings. We address this through Ballad (Bandit-based LLM Routing for Automated Heuristic Discovery), an online bandit algorithm that learns to orchestrate a pool of LLMs into an ensemble that matches or exceeds what any soloist LLM achieves alone. This orchestration is powered by an LLM selection policy that favors rare, high-scoring heuristics over stable averages, a DAG-based credit propagation mechanism through the genealogy of the heuristic population, and discounting of stale observations to remain attuned to each model's current utility. Across five NP-hard combinatorial optimization problems and a pool of four models of varying capability and cost, Ballad not only matches the best individual LLM in hindsight but, in several cases, surpasses every individual model while reducing API cost by up to 65.6% relative to always using the strongest model.

Al-Ghasali: A Computational Framework for Simulating Islamic Jurisprudential Rulings via Finite State Automata and Large Language Models
Authors: Elnaserledinellah Mahmoud Abdelwahab
First: 2026-11-12T18:23:56+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-12T18:23:56+00:00
Comments: 18 pages, 1 figure
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
This paper presents Al Ghasali, a computational framework designed to formalize and simulate Islamic jurisprudential (Fiqh) rulings. Building upon recent set theoretic formalizations of al sabr wa al Taqsim that extract minimal operational rules as simple Horn clause logical programs, this work proves that the semantics of closed Fiqh chapters collapse completely to deterministic Finite State Automata (FSA). We establish a formal computational hierarchy, demonstrating that restricting formalization to finite propositional Horn theories guarantees decidability and constant time (O(1)) verdict extraction after offline compilation. The framework formally proves that hierarchical compositions of sub-chapter FSAs preserve this O(1) complexity, and that temporal juristic requirements, such as Tartib (order), are deterministically flattened into static ontological states, preserving the memoryless property of the automaton. To generate human-readable juristic summaries, Al Ghasali incorporates Large Language Models (LLMs) via an architecture where the deterministic FSA strictly controls the probabilistic LLM. Crucially, the paper formulates sufficient design safety conditions for this FSA LLM integration, ensuring strict adherence to the epistemological boundaries of Usul al Fiqh. To neutralize the risk of LLM hallucination, the system employs a Grammar Guided Constrained Decoding mechanism. We formally define the mapping function that dynamically constructs unambiguous right linear grammars from the FSA execution history. The paper mathematically proves that this architecture, augmented by a Bounded Neutral Lexicon and Valence Locking, guarantees the absolute absence of Decisional Hallucination, Juristic Factual Hallucination, and Semantic Inversion reducing the LLM to a pure linguistic transformer of deterministic juristic facts

The Qualitative Laboratory: Theory Prototyping and Hypothesis Generation with Large Language Models
Authors: Hugues Draelants
First: 2025-11-25T08:31:48+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-12T18:19:34+00:00
Comments: 27 pages, 3 tables. Unpublished manuscript
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
The use of Large Language Models (LLMs) to simulate social actors has garnered attention for its implications for the epistemic status of discourses generated through such means. This paper considers the more modest question of whether sociological role-playing can serve as a heuristic instrument in the generation of qualitative hypotheses. We take as a case study the development of four divergent fictional characters using a theoretically informed Matrix of Climate Reception. These characters are asked to respond to a series of discourse frames covering two topics: a proposed ban on the sale of new gasoline-powered cars, and the inclusion of climate justice in the school curriculum. We then analyse the 32 model-generated responses for their commonalities, situational differences and divergences from the presuppositions underlying the characters' profiles. The experiment illustrates how LLMs can be used to flesh out theoretical presuppositions into contextual narratives and to propose avenues of further inquiry. It also shows that role-playing can generate considerable similarity between characters and is sensitive to how prompts are constructed, warning against the confusion of discursive coherence with sociological data. Role-playing is argued to be just one of several possible ways to use LLMs for generating hypotheses, with the heuristic specificity of this procedure compared with other methods of direct LLM-based reasoning or with other techniques remaining an open issue. The qualitative laboratory is thus conceived of as a heuristic instrument for provoking reflection and refining questions, not for testing theories with artificial data.

LEAP: Supercharging LLMs for Formal Mathematics with Agentic Frameworks
Authors: Po-Nien Kung, Linfeng Song, Dawsen Hwang, Jinsung Yoon, Chun-Liang Li, Simone Severini, Mirek Olšák, Edward Lockhart, Quoc V Le, Burak Gokturk, Thang Luong, Tomas Pfister, Nanyun Peng
First: 2026-06-02T08:16:42+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-12T18:13:31+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) exhibit strong informal mathematical reasoning but struggle to generate mechanically verifiable proofs in formal languages like Lean. We present LEAP, an agentic framework that enables general-purpose foundation models to achieve state-of-the-art performance on automated formal theorem proving. LEAP leverages foundation model capabilities, such as informal reasoning, instruction following, and iterative self-refinement. By decomposing complex problems into smaller units, the system bridges formal proof construction with informal blueprints through continuous interaction with the Lean compiler. To provide a rigorous evaluation beyond increasingly saturated benchmarks, we introduce Lean-IMO-Bench, a benchmark of IMO-style problems formalized in Lean, with short statements yet highly non-routine and multi-step proofs across a wide range of difficulty levels. Empirically, on the latest 2025 Putnam Competition, an annual mathematics competition for undergraduate students in North America, LEAP solves all 12 problems, matching recent breakthroughs by frontier formal mathematical models. On Lean-IMO-Bench, LEAP boosts the one-shot formal solve rate of general-purpose LLMs from below 10% to 70%, notably surpassing the 48% benchmark set by a specialized, gold-medal-caliber IMO system. Furthermore, we demonstrate LEAP's research-level utility by autonomously formalizing complex proofs for open combinatorial challenges, including a verified proof for a key subproblem in Knuth's Hamiltonian decomposition of even-order Cayley graphs.

Patient-specific rubric-based evaluation of large language models across longitudinal cancer care
Authors: Wei-Wei Zhang, Pengkai Wang, Yan Li, Zhijie Sang, Guan-Qun Zhou, Jun-Yan Li, Shu-Han Zhou, Pei-Wen Liu, Ting-Xi Tang, Min Tang, Zhitian Hou, Jianmin Wu, Jian Guan, Li Lin, Hongxia Yang, Ying Sun

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) show growing promise in specialty medicine, yet existing evaluations largely focus on isolated clinical encounters rather than assessing the distinct clinical responsibilities along longitudinal care pathways. We introduce NPC-RubricBench, a phase-aware benchmark for evaluating multistage clinical reasoning across the cancer care continuum, using nasopharyngeal carcinoma as a defined disease context. Curated from real-world records of 405 patients at two cancer centers, it comprises 941 evaluation units across six sequential care stages, from work-up through follow-up, and 17,688 patient-specific criteria across five dimensions. Each assessment is restricted to clinical evidence available at the corresponding decision point. Across 30 general-purpose and medical LLMs, we find strong diagnostic scores did not ensure reliable downstream care. Decision Quality ranged from 56.8 to 94.7 in diagnosis on a 0-100 scale, but dropped to 8.9-56.0 in staging and spanned 32.4-94.2 in treatment. Crucially, failure modes differed across phases: explicit decision errors predominated in anatomical staging (48.2%), whereas decision omissions were more frequent during initial work-up (33.4%) and post-treatment follow-up (30.4%). Varying query granularity revealed a trade-off between decision quality and other clinical requirements. Broad open-ended requests reduced decision quality across all 30 models, whereas more detailed, checklist-like prompts improved decision quality but were accompanied by lower evidence faithfulness and safety adherence. These findings highlight the need to assess clinical reliability against the distinct clinical requirements of each phase along the longitudinal care pathway. Finally, NPC-RubricBench informs the design of stage-aware clinical guardrails and offers a reusable blueprint for evaluating LLMs across other cancers and chronic diseases.

CS-54k: A Paper-Grounded Corpus and Benchmark for AI Research QA

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being used to support computer science research. To contribute reliably, these systems must be able to work with information distributed across research papers and across different aspects of the research process. However, large-scale resources that systematically organize such information into structured supervision for both model evaluation and post-training remain limited. To address this gap, we propose a systematic paper-to-QA construction pipeline that organizes information from AI research papers into structured QA pairs supported by source evidence. Using this pipeline, we construct CS-54k, a 54k-pair corpus derived from 14,474 openly licensed AI research papers. The corpus covers eight recurring paper components, including research domain, prior work, existing challenges, motivation, findings and assumptions, methods, experimental settings, and experimental results. We further derive two complementary subsets: CS-4k, a benchmark for evaluating LLMs on paper-derived research QA, and CS-50k, a companion corpus for post-training. Extensive evaluations on CS-4k reveal substantial performance differences across existing LLMs and paper components, with experimental settings and results remaining particularly challenging. Moreover, post-training on CS-50k substantially improves Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct on CS-4k, demonstrating the practical utility of structured, paper-derived supervision. Together, CS-4k and CS-50k provide a unified and scalable resource for benchmarking and improving LLMs on research paper question answering.

Abstract
Ontology matching (OM) enables semantic interoperability between different ontologies and resolves their conceptual heterogeneity by aligning related entities. OM systems currently have two prevailing design paradigms: conventional knowledge-based expert systems and newer machine learning-based predictive systems. While large language models (LLMs) and LLM agents have revolutionised data engineering and have been applied creatively in many domains, their potential for OM remains underexplored. This study introduces a novel agent-powered LLM-based design paradigm for OM systems. With consideration of several specific challenges in leveraging LLM agents for OM, we propose a generic framework, namely Agent-OM (Agent for Ontology Matching), consisting of two Siamese agents for retrieval and matching, with a set of OM tools. Our framework is implemented in a proof-of-concept system. Evaluations of three Ontology Alignment Evaluation Initiative (OAEI) tracks over state-of-the-art OM systems show that our system can achieve results very close to the long-standing best performance on simple OM tasks and can significantly improve the performance on complex and few-shot OM tasks.

Auditing AI peer reviewers: a benchmark on real scientific papers
Authors: Íñigo Zubeldia, Boris Bolliet, Francisco Villaescusa-Navarro, Pablo Villanueva-Domingo, Miles Cranmer
Venue: NeurIPS 2026
First: 2026-11-12T16:33:51+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-12T16:33:51+00:00

Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
LLM-based peer review is now used at production scale, but its evaluation remains limited. We introduce a benchmark that injects objective, localisable errors of four types (general issues, mathematics, numerics, citations) at three severity levels into real papers and measures each reviewer's detection rate by error type and severity, together with its false-positive rate on clean copies. We also present Skepthical, a multi-agent reviewer that verifies citation-backed claims by retrieving the cited papers, mathematics with computer algebra, and numerical claims with executed code. We apply the benchmark to two corpora of 15 recent arXiv preprints each, one in astrophysics and one in machine learning (ML), injecting a total of 357 errors, and evaluate twelve reviewer systems: six single-LLM reviewers, two Claude Code configurations, three publicly available reviewers, and Skepthical. The choice of model has the largest impact on detection rate: given the same prompt, three frontier LLMs differ by about 50 percentage points in total detection on both corpora. Tools and scaffolding help when they contribute what the model cannot supply on its own. In particular, on astrophysics, Claude Code improves on a single Claude call in mathematics and numerics detection, and enforced citation retrieval gives Skepthical the highest citation error detection rate of all systems by a large margin. This citation lead, however, is not observed on ML, where the models appear to know the literature better and Skepthical's citation detection rate is statistically indistinguishable from that of the best single LLM. False-positive rates are below 10% for most systems, but vary with prompt, model, and scaffolding, with mathematics flags the main source of false positives. The evaluation pipeline, the injected errors, and the adjudication logs are released as a benchmark.

AURA 2.0: From User-Driven Responses to Model-Initiated Conversations in Streaming Video

Abstract
Most AI assistants respond only when prompted, remaining silent even when events in the video stream offer meaningful opportunities for conversation. To move beyond purely user-driven interaction, we present AURA 2.0, a streaming video large language model that introduces three core upgrades to AURA. The key advance is Model-Initiated Conversation (MIC), which enables the model to identify topics worth discussing in the evolving visual stream and initiate conversations at appropriate moments without a user query. Stream-Aware Tool Calling (SATC) enables the model to invoke external tools using the current visual and conversational context and incorporate returned information into the ongoing interaction. Interactive Visual Grounding (IVG) supports visual grounding in both user inputs and model outputs. The model can identify objects marked by users on video frames and produce frame-aligned bounding boxes to link its responses to specific objects and regions. To develop these capabilities, we construct a dedicated streaming interaction dataset and adopt a three-stage training scheme to integrate the new behaviors with AURA's existing streaming interaction abilities. We further incorporate Long-Horizon Interaction Memory (LHIM) to maintain contextual continuity over extended video streams. Evaluations on public streaming benchmarks show that AURA 2.0 achieves leading performance among open-source streaming models, while targeted evaluations further validate its new interaction capabilities. We open-source the model weights and inference code.

Infrastructure for Verifiable AI: Where Proof Costs Go and How Hardware Can Help
Authors: Yihan Xia, Wenxin Xu, Taotao Wang
First: 2026-11-12T16:27:07+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-12T16:27:07+00:00
Comments: 16 pages, 8 figures, 5 tables
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Users of artificial intelligence services, including those powered by large language models and other transformer-based models, need assurance that inference and training follow the declared computation without exposing private models or data. Zero-knowledge proofs can provide this assurance, but proof generation remains expensive. Hardware acceleration research often reports gains for individual cryptographic operations, leaving their effect on the full cost of proving neural-network workloads, particularly large transformer models, unclear. We introduce a four-layer framework linking verifiable AI workloads, proof systems, cryptographic kernels and hardware platforms, use it to review the literature, and add original measurements of six provers and seven kernels to identify which costs matter in practice. Our measurements show that the dominant cost changes with the proof system and input length. In low-rank fine-tuning, the cost of opening commitments to fixed model weights remains nearly constant across input lengths from 64 to 2048, while the work associated with intermediate values grows. A 13-billion-parameter fine-tuning configuration exceeds the capacity of a 40-gigabyte graphics processor but completes on an 80-gigabyte device, showing why capacity must be assessed alongside execution time. Our kernel profiling further identifies instruction issue and synchronization limits that a bandwidth-only model misses. We use these findings to assess graphics processors, reconfigurable logic, custom chips and existing artificial intelligence accelerators. The resulting research agenda connects proof-friendly numeric formats, reuse of fixed-weight commitments, heterogeneous execution and shared benchmarks to the measured bottlenecks. Effective acceleration requires selecting hardware and proof-system optimizations around the cost profile of the target workload.

From Delegation to Coordination: Learning Efficient Multi-Agent Deep Research
Authors: Yuyang Wu, Yang Liu, Zequn Xie, Junjie Wang, Dan Yang, Zehao Tan, Ding-Chu Zhang, Junming Yang, Yue Shen, Jian Wang, Jinjie Gu
First: 2026-11-12T16:26:27+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-12T16:26:27+00:00
Comments: 16 pages, 3 figures, 3 tables
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Multi-agent deep-research systems use delegation to parallelize search and keep long tool traces out of the main agent's context. Yet delegation helps only when task allocation and subtask execution are coordinated: the main agent must issue useful subtasks, while subagents must return reliable evidence. Existing training either optimizes only the main agent or broadcasts the same team advantage to all agents, which can reward misleading subagent reports and penalize useful ones. We introduce LEAD-RL (Learning Effective Agent Delegation with Reinforcement Learning), a joint main-agent-subagent training framework with contribution-aware credit assignment. A training-time rubric evaluates the research utility of each subagent report and gates its inherited team advantage, while final-answer correctness remains the only team reward; no token, step, or speedup reward is used. Across five benchmarks and two model families, LEAD-RL improves answer quality. On BrowseComp, it improves accuracy by 2.49 percentage points and reduces critical-path cost by 24.5% over joint training with shared team credit. During validation, accuracy increases by 6.78 points at approximately constant critical-path cost. These results show that contribution-aware credit assignment improves the quality-efficiency trade-off of multi-agent deep research.

Maximizing LLM Utility While Managing LLM Fallibility
Authors: Ekta U. Samani, Christopher G. Atkeson
First: 2026-11-12T16:09:49+00:00 · Latest: 2026-11-12T16:09:49+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) can supply autonomous systems with plans, predictions, and judgments, but these contributions are sometimes wrong. This review asks how a system can maximize an LLM's utility while managing its fallibility. Our answer is to adapt the system's reliance on the LLM as evidence about the LLM's performance accumulates. Systems built from imperfect components, such as noisy sensors and faulty software, have long worked in this way, and the classical methods they use can be applied to LLM-based systems as well. A system can change its reliance on an LLM in several ways, depending on the LLM's role and on the other components in the system. Each change needs an estimate of the LLM's reliability, and the LLM's role and the kind of contribution it makes determine what to estimate. Classical methods from decision theory, estimation, and online learning then turn these estimates into reliance decisions. These decisions can reduce how often LLM errors affect the system. However, some errors matter even when they are rare. An independently verified mechanism can prevent such errors from being executed. Without one, adapting reliance using reliability estimates can still keep their risk within a bound. Studies on adapting reliance on LLMs are emerging but limited, and the reviewed comparisons suggest that adapting reliance can improve system performance.

Abstract
Artificial intelligence (AI) is changing how biological research is done. Biological foundation models trained on millions of nucleotides, protein, and single-cell measurements predict protein structures, mutation effects, and cellular responses to genetic perturbations. Agentic systems, AI assistants built on large language models, can run multistep analyses from plain-language requests and increasingly call foundation models as tools. Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents use external skills to reuse task procedures and experience with tools. General-purpose skills describe shared execution procedures, while specific tool choices, operating practices, and output requirements vary across users. In long-horizon tasks, subgoals, available inputs, and environmental states change as execution progresses. Agents must select currently applicable skills from an expanding skill pool and prepare candidates for subsequent steps. We propose Skill Adapter, which enables personalized modification and online routing of general-purpose skills. The system distills local adjustments from user requirements and execution feedback and stores them as Adapters linked to general-purpose skills. The routing agent selects general-purpose skills and applicable Adapters based on the current subtask, execution progress, and environmental feedback, incrementally maintaining a task-level online skill library that the main LLM reads on demand. The system preloads candidates for subsequent nodes and rechecks their applicability once actual inputs are determined. Reinforcement learning optimizes the two types of decisions using modification gains and routing outcomes, respectively. We evaluate Skill Adapter on ALFWorld, WebShop, and SkillsBench. Compared with SkillRL, it improves task success rates by an average of 11.43 percentage points and reduces mean execution time on all three benchmarks. Skill Adapter retains its performance advantage when the skill pool grows to 1,000 skills.

SPADE: Soft Prompts for Ai-text DEtection
Authors: Alex Jiang, Didier Schwab, Chuyuan Li

Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
The easy access to Large Language Models (LLMs) has made AI-generated texts on the web increasingly ubiquitous, making AI-text detection a crucial task. Prompts greatly influence the generation process, but existing detectors focus solely on the generated texts --- supervised methods learn from labeled texts only, and zero-shot methods rely on the text's probability distributions --- ignoring the underlying generation process, which limits their robustness in open-world scenarios. We introduce SPADE: Soft Prompts for Ai-text DEtection, a novel framework for detecting AI-generated text that transfers better across datasets. Specifically, we measure the prompt's influence and use it as a discriminative signal. We characterize the prompt's influence by introducing a Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) term that captures how much a prompt explains a text. Based on this quantity, SPADE trains one soft prompt per class to amplify the separation, and predicts the class whose prompt best explains the candidate text. Extensive experiments on several benchmarks against various detectors demonstrate the robustness and effectiveness of our method, achieving state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance on out-of-distribution (OOD) datasets.

Abstract
Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly support long-horizon tasks by leveraging external memory and past experience. Abstract
Second-order optimizers (e.g., Shampoo, SOAP) have emerged as compelling alternatives to Adam/AdamW and promise faster per-step convergence than AdamW in transformer training. Abstract

Abstract
Predictive tasks constitute subprocedures within human--AI interactions (HAIs) involving professionals and AI systems. Complementarity occurs when an HAI outperforms a benchmark defined by its constituent agents. Existing formalizations, however, focus on a single human and a single AI, leaving multi-agent interactions insufficiently characterized. We introduce a tree-based mathematical framework for multi-agent complementarity. Ordered agent-role configurations decorate the leaves of rooted planar binary trees and local aggregation rules generate protocol outputs evaluated against either a best-average or a pointwise-min benchmark. We establish four results. First, under linear pooling, every protocol tree provides a bijective coordinate system for the interior of the simplex of global leaf weights. Arbitrary trees with the same ordered leaves admit output- and complementarity-preserving reparameterizations, realized locally through path-independent Tamari rotations. However, HAI constraints can violate admissibility preservation between equivalent protocols. Second, for regression under squared loss, we characterize complementarity through residual correction and derive optimal linear-pooling weights. We also characterize protocol-indifference loci under identical local parameters. Third, selector-based protocols cannot achieve complementarity relative to the pointwise-min benchmark, independently of task and loss. Fourth, in binary classification, HAIs cannot achieve complementarity under endpoint-monotone losses and the pointwise-min benchmark, with an analogous obstruction for multiclass cross-entropy. Numerical illustrations examine the main claims. We provide a mathematical foundation for analyzing multi-agent predictive subprocedures within HAIs, establishing a first step toward understanding how interaction, aggregation, and real-world constraints affect HAI performance.

Abstract
Long-context inference with Large Language Models (LLMs) is bottlenecked by the linearly growing memory of the key-value (KV) cache. Existing compression methods reduce the cache through token eviction or approximation, but degrade sharply at aggressive compression budgets. We propose PatchKV, a training-free framework that compensates KV cache compression methods by carrying part of the context in the model's weights. PatchKV pairs an off-the-shelf compressed KV cache with a context-specific weight patch, which is computed once at context-loading time and served for downstream queries for the context. The weight patch is derived in closed form via ridge regression, by aligning the block-wise activations of context-derived reference query tokens under the full cache and the compressed cache. Once merged into the model, the patch leaves the forward graph and per-query inference cost unchanged in the single-context, multi-query setting. Across long-context QA (SCBench with up to 170K tokens, SQuAD, NIAH) and math (GSM8K) benchmarks on three model architectures, PatchKV consistently improves cache compression methods, suggesting an alternative direction to compensate them at aggressive budgets.
